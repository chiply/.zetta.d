** What this is

Three questions, in order:

1. What does each system actually prescribe?  Part 1: every system in
   under a hundred words, plus its primitives, rituals, rules and cost.
2. Where do they overlap, where do they contradict, and what survives?
   Part 2: the concept crosswalk, the convergent core ranked by how many
   systems arrive at it, the tensions with a resolution for this setup,
   and the composite system stated in full.  This is the bulk.
3. How does the composite land on org + Hyperbole with the least
   bookkeeping?  Part 3: what already exists, what is missing ranked by
   leverage, the AI decoration contract, and the cadence table.

** The requirement, restated as design constraints

From the ask: "minimum bookkeeping.  i should be focused on writing tasks.
AI and Emacs tooling fill in the gaps."  Writing tasks is the one kind of
writing the system asks of you: the work, in your own words, when you
think of it.  Everything else about a task is somebody else's job.  Every
recommendation below is checked against these eight:

- *C1 Capture is raw, and writing tasks is your only writing.*  A
  captured item costs one gesture and zero decisions: the work in your
  own words, when you think of it.  Everything else is added later, by
  software or by an LLM, and only confirmed by you.  Every system in Part
  1 already splits the write from the reading; the composite makes the
  split absolute.
- *C2 Derived, not typed.*  Any field that can be computed from evidence
  you already leave behind (the state log, which is also the clock once
  G15 assembles intervals from it, =:CREATED:=, the kb's git timeline,
  calendar, mail, links) is computed.  You type only
  what no evidence can tell: whether you want a thing, and why.
- *C3 Keep the re-decision, drop the friction.*  The pruning the analog
  systems buy with rewriting (Bullet Journal migration, Autofocus
  dismissal, Ivy Lee's six) is supplied instead by views that put age,
  times carried and last touch in front of you at a fixed cadence.
- *C4 One corpus, many lenses.*  Tasks, notes, annotations, journal and
  wiki are all text under =~/kb=; there is no second store.  Views (agenda,
  queue, rolo, wiki graph, irs) are the organisation.  Folders are not.
- *C5 The plan is a proposal.*  Software proposes (=org-queue=), you dispose
  (=NEXT=).  Nothing is silently dropped; every exclusion carries a reason.
- *C6 A habit is not a task.*  Recurring behaviour is tracked as a strength
  series with forgiveness built in, never as a pile of overdue tasks.
- *C7 Testable.*  This one is not from the ask; it is the repo's own
  convention, set by =org-queue-core= and its 41 batch tests.  Every
  proposed mechanism has a batch test in =emacs -Q=, so that what you
  build to escape bookkeeping does not become bookkeeping of its own.
  If it cannot be tested that way, it is a view, not a mechanism, and
  is labelled as such.
- *C8 You are the constraint.*  From the follow-up on agent work, not
  the original ask.  Most code is now written by an agent you kick off
  and review; your synchronous attention for specifying and reviewing is
  the scarce resource, and agent time is capacity, not time.  Plans
  count human minutes; the number of chains in flight is capped by what
  you can review; landings are batched, never served.

** The composite in one paragraph

Capture everything and decide nothing at capture.  A separate sitting
turns each raw item into a commitment or an option: the LLM proposes
kind, area, context, effort and dates against the config's closed lists,
and you confirm in a keystroke.  Work is chosen by a computed queue
against a measured capacity, inside a routine that fixes the shape of the
day; you make one to three picks and override with =NEXT=.  A deadline is
a fact, importance is set once at intake, the running order is derived.
Three things in progress, and at most three agent chains in flight,
each one prompt ahead, their landings reviewed at unit boundaries rather
than as they arrive.  The day is closed by a generated checklist,
tomorrow's picks and tomorrow's prompts.  The weekly review is a report the system writes and
you answer, and it is where "still worth it?" is asked with the evidence
attached.  Above the week sit a theme that cannot fail and at most three
missions a season.  Habits live in their own store as a strength score
with forgiveness built in.  Notes climb from fleeting to permanent in your
own words with a reason on every link, and come back by scheduler or by
linking.  Nothing is rewritten by hand; anything the state log (which
is the clock, assembled from transitions), the calendar, mail or git
can say is derived rather than typed.

* Part 1 — The systems, concisely

Forty-four systems in six families, each in the same shape so they can be
read against one another: what it solves and its one characteristic move;
its nouns; its verbs and their cadence; its named rules; what it costs to
keep by hand and where it prunes; and the one thing no other system here
has.  Codes in brackets are used throughout the rest of the document.

** The action-management lineage

*** [GTD] Getting Things Done (David Allen, 2001; revised 2015)
Every unfinished commitment is an "open loop" occupying short-term memory
until captured somewhere the mind trusts more than itself; the promised
state is "mind like water".  It optimises for control and perspective and
deliberately not for prioritisation, which is left to intuition in the
moment.  It is bottom-up: clear the runway before thinking about purpose.
Its characteristic move is the clarify flowchart, a runnable algorithm from
free text to next action, project, waiting-for, calendar, someday or trash.
The 2015 edition renamed the steps and horizons; the mechanics are
unchanged.
- Primitives :: In (capture buckets); next action; project (multi-step outcome); next-action lists by context; Waiting For; calendar as hard landscape; tickler, Someday/Maybe, reference; Horizons of Focus (six levels)
- Rituals :: capture continuously; clarify whenever In empties (actionable? two minutes? delegate? defer?); choose by context, time, energy, then priority; daily calendar and tickler; eleven-step weekly review (Get Clear, Get Current, Get Creative); monthly areas, quarterly goals, yearly vision, Natural Planning Model per project
- Rules :: two-minute rule; a next action is "physical and visible"; the calendar is sacred, no daily to-do lists; "you can't decide priority in a vacuum"
- Cost :: High: each finished action means deciding and writing the next, Waiting For must be dated and chased, and the weekly review re-reads every list, commonly one to two hours; pruning happens only at clarify time and in the Someday/Maybe review, since Allen wants the Projects list complete, not short.
- Distinct :: The next action plus context as the atomic unit, and a checklisted weekly review with one machine-checkable invariant, every project has a next action.

*** [ZTD] Zen to Done (Leo Babauta, 2007; ebook 2008)
GTD reduced to ten habits adopted one at a time, with Covey's big rocks and
a short daily list bolted on.  Written as a critique: GTD demands many habit
changes at once, rewards filing over doing, is too unstructured, captures
indiscriminately and ignores goals.  It optimises for finishing one to three
important things a day with the simplest tools; Minimal ZTD keeps four
habits, a notebook and a pen.  Its characteristic move is the MIT: one to
three Most Important Tasks, chosen each morning, as the entire daily plan.
- Primitives :: notebook, always carried; context lists, as few as possible; calendar with Big Rocks; Big Rocks, four to six weekly; MITs, one to three daily; single yearly goal; short-term goal
- Rituals :: capture, one task at a time; process inboxes daily with the two-minute rule; set MITs each morning, do the first before email; plan tomorrow each evening; weekly: choose Big Rocks, 30-minute five-step review; monthly goal review, yearly life goals
- Rules :: MITs first, early; big rocks before gravel, one or two a day; "the simpler the tools, the better"; cut the list in half, then cut again
- Cost :: Low by design, one to three lines each morning, a 30-minute weekly pass and notebook transcription; pruning is a named habit, Simplify: halve the list, drop stale items, say no.
- Distinct :: Habit-at-a-time adoption with an explicit behaviour-change protocol, and simplification as a scheduled habit.

*** [FTF] First Things First and The 7 Habits (Stephen R. Covey, 1989; with the Merrills, 1994)
Effectiveness comes from doing what is important rather than what is urgent,
importance judged against a personal mission and the roles you hold: compass
before clock, Covey's "fourth generation" after checklists, calendars and
prioritised goal-setting.  It optimises for time in Quadrant II, important
but not urgent, where planning, prevention, relationships and renewal live.
Its characteristic move is weekly planning by role: for each role ask what
one thing this week would make the most difference, and book those big rocks
before anything else.
- Primitives :: time management matrix, four quadrants; personal mission statement; roles, about seven; weekly goals, one or two per role; Quadrant II organizer worksheet; four dimensions of renewal
- Rituals :: weekly, 20 to 30 minutes: reconnect with the mission, list roles, choose big rocks, book them first; daily ten minutes adapting the day; in the moment, ask which quadrant and refuse QIII; end-of-week evaluation; renewal across body, heart, mind, spirit
- Rules :: "schedule your priorities; don't prioritise your schedule"; big rocks first, then gravel; "the enemy of the best is often the good"
- Cost :: Moderate and repetitive: five to fifteen goals re-derived weekly from a stable role list and re-prioritised daily, with the Franklin Planner adding ABC codes; QIII and QIV are pruned explicitly, but there is no inbox and no home for actions neither dated nor tied to a weekly goal.
- Distinct :: Roles as the axis of planning, the week as the unit, and renewal as scheduled work rather than leftover time.

*** [EIS] The Eisenhower matrix as commonly practised (grid from Covey, 1989; verb-per-cell form, 2010s)
A triage grid: each task is judged urgent or not and important or not, and
each cell carries a verb, Do first, Schedule, Delegate, Delete.  Its
authority is Eisenhower's 1954 remark that "the urgent are not important,
and the important are never urgent", which he credited to a former college
president; Eisenhower drew no grid, Covey did in 1989.  Where Covey's matrix
diagnoses where a life's time goes, the popular version sorts each task on
arrival, and by putting "Do first" on urgency it legitimises what Covey
wanted shrunk.
- Primitives :: four cells with verbs; urgent, a near consequence; important, serves goals; cap of eight per cell; one combined list; delegation log
- Rituals :: sort each new task on arrival; clear Q1 today; date Q2 into the calendar; hand off Q3, note the follow-up; delete Q4; weekly re-sort as Q2 drifts to Q1
- Rules :: do, then schedule, then delegate, then don't do; eight per cell; "it is not about collecting but finishing tasks"; if everything is Q1, you are planning too little
- Cost :: Two yes-or-no judgements per task, re-made whenever urgency changes, plus a delegated list and a periodic redraw; Q4 prunes, but importance is rarely revisited and Q2 has no forcing function beyond a date.
- Distinct :: A single memorable two-by-two with an imperative verb in each cell, an explicit delete cell and numeric caps.

*** [IVY] The Ivy Lee method (Ivy Ledbetter Lee, 1918 by tradition)
At the end of each workday write the six most important things for tomorrow,
rank them, and next morning work them strictly in order, one at a time,
carrying the remainder forward.  It optimises for starting: the decision is
made the night before and a hard cap forces ranking.  The
Schwab-pays-$25,000 story has no contemporary record; the earliest tellings
date from 1960 and Clear's 1918 rests on a 1994 history.  Treat it as a
parable with a good rule inside: a hard cap, forced ranking, serial
execution.
- Primitives :: the list of six; rank 1 to 6; carry-over
- Rituals :: evening: write and rank six; morning onward: item one until finished, then item two; close: move the remainder to tomorrow's list
- Rules :: six, no more; one thing at a time, in order; decide the night before; carry over rather than pile on
- Cost :: Six lines a day, where the evening re-ranking is the re-deciding; there is no backlog and no pruning rule, so anything that never makes the six does not exist to the method, which is why practitioners bolt on a master list.
- Distinct :: A hard numeric cap with forced ranking and strictly serial execution, and carry-over as the only backlog.

*** [FROG] Eat That Frog! (Brian Tracy, 2001; third edition 2017)
An anti-procrastination manual.  The frog is your biggest, most important
task, the one you are most likely to procrastinate on; eat it first thing,
and if there are two, the ugliest first. Importance is defined by
consequences: a task matters to the degree that not doing it has serious
potential consequences.  It optimises for starting the consequential thing
early.  Its characteristic move is the ABCDE list, re-labelled every day,
with the rule that you never do a B while an A is undone.  The Twain
attribution is unsupported.
- Primitives :: the frog, A-1; ABCDE list; key result areas, five to seven; law of three; three questions
- Rituals :: evening: tomorrow's list marked A to E, As numbered; morning: A-1 single-handled to completion; in the moment: "do it now", salami-slice or Swiss-cheese; periodic: key result areas, law of three, key constraint
- Rules :: eat the frog first; never a B before an A; "there is never enough time to do everything, but always enough for the most important thing"; if nothing happens when you don't do it, don't
- Cost :: A daily list re-labelled every day from master, monthly and weekly lists plus occasional analyses; E and creative procrastination prune explicitly, but there is no inbox and nothing says what becomes of the Cs that never die.
- Distinct :: Frog-first as a sequencing rule and consequences as the operational test of importance.

** The Cal Newport corpus

*** [DW] Deep Work (Cal Newport, 2016)
Deep work is distraction-free concentration that pushes cognitive capability
to its limit; shallow work is logistical and often done while distracted.
Network tools make busyness the default, so the system optimises for hours
of unbroken concentration aimed at few goals, valued per hour.  Four rules:
work deeply, embrace boredom, quit social media, drain the shallows.  Its
characteristic move is accountability by metric rather than list: a
scoreboard of deep hours as the lead measure, borrowed from 4DX, and a
spoken shutdown phrase that closes the day's loops.
- Primitives :: depth philosophy, monastic to journalistic; deep work ritual; deep-hours tally, the scoreboard; shallow-work budget, 30–50%; depth score of a task; fixed schedule, a stop time; internet blocks; sender filter and email protocol
- Rituals :: run the start ritual; one deep block daily at the same hour; shutdown ritual at the stop time, ending "Shutdown complete"; productive meditation on walks; weekly cadence of accountability against the tally; deep blocks reserved four weeks ahead
- Rules :: about four deep hours a day is the ceiling; "take breaks from focus", not from distraction; batch shallow work against attention residue; adopt a tool only if it substantially helps what matters
- Cost :: A tally card, a shutdown checklist whose cost scales with the task inventory, and the daily grid; pruning happens by refusal, budgets, stop time and filters, never by list hygiene.
- Distinct :: A lead-measure scoreboard for hours of focus, a depth score for activities, and a spoken sentinel phrase that closes the day.

*** [TBP] Time-block planning (Cal Newport; blog 2007–2013, planner 2020)
A to-do list omits how long each task takes and where the day's free time
is, so "to-do lists are a terrible daily planning tool".  Instead "give
every minute a job" in advance, on paper, and revise the plan rather than
abandon it when the day diverges.  It optimises for realism about finite
time and for protecting deep blocks.  Its characteristic move is the
revision column: when the plan breaks, cross out the remainder and redraw it
beside the original, so the re-plan is the practice, not a failure.
- Primitives :: daily spread, a two-page grid; block, minimum 30 minutes; revision columns; collection columns, tasks and ideas; daily metrics line; weekly planning spread
- Rituals :: morning: consult the weekly plan, calendar and boards, draw the blocks; in the moment: interruptions into the collection columns, a slip triggers a redraw; evening: record metrics, move collection items into the system, shutdown; weekly: fill the spread from the quarterly plan; quarterly: a new planner
- Rules :: every minute gets a job, but the plan is a hypothesis; leave slack beside the blocks; nothing under 30 minutes gets its own block; metrics are "a training regime for your will"
- Cost :: One hand-drawn schedule a day plus one to three redraws, calendar events re-copied each morning, collection items re-copied at night and a nightly metrics line; the planner prunes nothing and is consumed quarterly.
- Distinct :: Revision columns as a first-class artefact, capture columns bound to the plan page, and habit metrics recorded beside the schedule.

*** [MSP] Multi-scale planning (Cal Newport; blog 2014–2015, Deep Questions 2020–2023)
Plan at three scales, quarterly or seasonal, weekly and daily, and read each
plan only at the scale below it, so the big picture influences every day
without daily thought.  It closes the gap between goals that never get hours
and lists that never confront time.  Its characteristic move is the weekly
template: standing rules, quotas, theme days and autopilot slots decided
once a quarter and kept apart from any week's plan.  Projects receive time
directly in the quarterly and weekly plans and are never decomposed into
task lists.
- Primitives :: strategic plan, one per role; weekly template: rules, quotas, themes; weekly plan, a narrative document; temporary multi-week plan; task boards, five columns per role; project versus task distinction
- Rituals :: daily: read the weekly plan and the boards, then time-block; weekly, over an hour: read plan, template, calendar and boards, write the narrative; quarterly: rewrite the plan and template, note what worked; the long view enters through lifestyle-centric planning
- Rules :: the cascade: which weeks, then which days, then which hours; a "keep your head above water" week is a legitimate plan; quotas and limits belong in the template; if the ideal week cannot hold the deep work, fix the role
- Cost :: Three living documents per role, with a weekly narrative that restates the template and re-copies quarterly intentions, accepted because writing forces confrontation with time; the quarterly rewrite is the only prune.
- Distinct :: Strict information hiding between planning scales, and projects that get time directly without ever becoming task lists.

*** [SP] Slow Productivity (Cal Newport, 2024)
Knowledge work never defined productivity, so it defaulted to
pseudo-productivity, visible activity as a proxy for effort.  Three
principles: do fewer things, work at a natural pace, obsess over quality; it
optimises for output over years rather than days.  Its characteristic move
is the simulated pull system: every commitment sits in an unbounded holding
tank, at most three are active, a freed slot pulls the next one in, and the
requester is told their queue position and an estimated date.  "Do fewer
things" is not "accomplish fewer things".
- Primitives :: missions, two or three; projects, limited by calendar hours; holding tank and active list; intake procedure with queue position; autopilot schedule; office hours, docket-clearing meeting; overhead tax per commitment; seasonality and cycles
- Rituals :: intake reply on every request; one deep project per day; office hours daily; docket cleared twice weekly; pull the next project when a slot frees; slow seasons and cooldowns yearly
- Rules :: three active projects at most; double the honest estimate; work 20% under capacity; "you cannot slay the dragon until you can see it"
- Cost :: One two-section project list whose estimated dates must be kept true and told to requesters, plus a five-year plan and a seasonal calendar; the lightest of his systems and the only one with an explicit prune, the active cap.
- Distinct :: A personal WIP limit with a designed social interface, and seasonality and cycles as planning primitives instead of uniform weeks.

*** [WWE] A World Without Email (Cal Newport, 2021)
The "hyperactive hive mind", coordination by unstructured, unscheduled
messages, is a workflow accident that squanders attention capital; the fix
is organisational, replacing conversation-driven coordination with designed
processes and protocols.  This is where Newport turns away from personal
productivity as the remedy.  The residue that transfers to one person is the
task board per role with a "Waiting to hear back" column, cards that hold
the whole conversation about a task, office hours for anything ambiguous,
and protocols authored as artefacts rather than etiquette hoped for.
- Primitives :: attention capital; task board per role; columns: to process, back burner, waiting, this week; card holding the conversation; office hours; scheduling protocol, three slots
- Rituals :: an email becomes a card at set times, not continuously; ambiguous asks go to office hours; weekly sweep of "Waiting to hear back" and "To process"; fill "This week" during weekly planning; revisit protocols quarterly
- Rules :: checking email less does not work, move the work out of email; one board per role, one role at a time; make the sender do the work; protocol pain now, coordination savings later
- Cost :: Every obligation becomes a card and every card needs column moves, with "Waiting to hear back" as manual state; back burner is the parking lot and has no purge rule.
- Distinct :: "Waiting to hear back" as a first-class column, and a protocol as an artefact you author.

*** [DM] Digital Minimalism (Cal Newport, 2019)
Only its time-management parts are in scope.  Optional technology eats
leisure by default, so leisure must be planned with the same structure as
work or it fills with low-quality consumption.  The machinery is a 30-day
declutter, a screen for readmitting a tool only with a written operating
procedure, and a seasonal-to-weekly leisure plan that mirrors the work
cascade.  Its characteristic move is scheduling low-quality leisure into a
fixed window rather than banning it.
- Primitives :: optional technology; operating procedure per tool; minimalist screen, three tests; seasonal leisure plan; weekly leisure plan; low-quality leisure window; fix-or-build project
- Rituals :: 30-day declutter once; weekly leisure plan; the scheduled indulgence window daily; seasonal rewrite and re-screen of every tool; weekly fix-or-build
- Rules :: "clutter is costly"; prioritise demanding activity over passive consumption; a readmitted tool must serve something deeply valued and be the best way to serve it; the real obstacle after a week is boredom
- Cost :: A rules sheet per tool, revisited seasonally, and two short leisure plans, the lowest of the six; it prunes by design, since reintroduction starts from a blank slate.
- Distinct :: Leisure planned with the same seasonal-to-weekly cascade as work, and tools admitted only with a written operating procedure.

** The knowledge-management lineage

*** [ZK] Zettelkasten (Niklas Luhmann; Sönke Ahrens, 2017; zettelkasten.de, 2013–2025)
Notes in your own words, each with a permanent address and explicit links,
kept for decades until the box reaches critical mass and returns ideas you
did not put in: Luhmann's "communication partner".  Ahrens: writing is the
medium in which thinking takes place, so the box is the writing project. It
optimises for long-run written output and surprise, not retrieval.  Its
characteristic move is link context, a stated reason on every link. Luhmann
never said "atomic"; Fast showed in 2025 that atomicity is a modern norm
read back into him.
- Primitives :: fleeting note, discarded within days; literature note; Zettel: one thought, permanent address; project note, discarded on shipping; Folgezettel branching numbers; link with link context; register of entry points; structure or hub note
- Rituals :: jot fleeting notes in the moment; while reading, note "page x says y"; daily: turn yesterday's notes into Zettels, place them, link both ways; when a cluster is thick, assemble a draft; no weekly cadence, review is a by-product of linking
- Rules :: "one knowledge building block per note"; placement does not matter if you can link; say why you link; own words only, "kept isn't read"
- Cost :: Every Zettel is a rewrite, a placement decision, two link sentences and often a register edit, none skippable because that work is the method; nothing is deleted from the box, pruning happens upstream in fleeting and project notes.
- Distinct :: A network designed to surprise its author, with a reason on every link and a sparse register of entry points instead of a tag index.

*** [EN] Evergreen notes (Andy Matuschak, 2019–present)
"'Better note-taking' misses the point; what matters is 'better thinking'":
notes written to evolve, contribute and accumulate across projects, with
evergreen notes written per day as the unit of knowledge work.  It optimises
for the author's own insight accreting, with spaced repetition as the
retention layer for other people's ideas.  Its characteristic move is the
title as API: a declarative claim as the handle, so notes compose, inside an
explicit ladder from daily log to writing inbox to evergreen note to
outline, with permission to delete.
- Primitives :: daily working log; writing inbox, drained mornings; reading inbox, triaged; evergreen note: atomic, concept-oriented, linked; outline note; proper-noun notes, books and people; spaced repetition prompt
- Rituals :: capture into the log; while reading, collect passages then "write about what you read"; the morning practice drains the writing inbox, most of the writing time; search old notes for links, which is the review; when an outline excites, fill gaps and rewrite; no weekly cadence
- Rules :: atomic, "only about one thing" but all of it; concept-oriented, not by source; densely linked, let structure emerge; "tags are an ineffective association structure"
- Cost :: Capture is free; each evergreen note is a rewrite, a claim-shaped title and a search-and-link pass, with renames rippling through links; pruning is explicit at the inbox and absent afterwards.
- Distinct :: Titles as APIs, an explicit maturity ladder with upward pressure, and spaced repetition embedded in the same medium.

*** [BASB] Building a Second Brain (Tiago Forte, 2022; blog 2017–2019)
Notes as raw material for deliverables: capture what resonates, organise by
actionability, distil opportunistically, express, because "you only know
what you make".  It optimises for output velocity through reuse, slow burns
instead of heavy lifts, not for thinking inside the notes.  Its
characteristic move is the intermediate packet, standalone work in progress
that snaps together like Lego, gated by Progressive Summarization, which
adds a compression layer only when you are already reviewing the note, and
filtered at capture by twelve favourite problems.
- Primitives :: CODE: capture, organize, distill, express; capture criteria: inspiring, useful, personal, surprising; intermediate packet; Progressive Summarization, five layers; 12 favorite problems; archipelago of ideas; Hemingway bridge; "dial down the scope"
- Rituals :: capture by resonance, no filing; distil only notes you touch; bridge at session end; weekly, 15–30 minutes: drain five inboxes and plan; monthly systems check of goals, projects, areas; project end: sweep for packets, archive
- Rules :: "projects over categories"; distil opportunistically; slow burns over heavy lifts; "keep your ideas moving"
- Cost :: Light at capture, with the debt deferred into review layers and PARA moves, a hard five-inbox weekly drain and a monthly hand-rewrite of project, area and goal lists; archives replace deletion and someday/maybe is the only pruning list.
- Distinct :: Intermediate packets and slow burns, a project-management stance on notes, and twelve favourite problems as a standing capture filter.

*** [PARA] PARA (Tiago Forte, 2017 blog series; 2023 book)
One organising axis, actionability, and four containers replicated
identically in every tool: projects (a goal, with an end), areas (a standard
to maintain, no end), resources (interest, not responsibility), archives
(inactive).  It answers "where do I put this" without a taxonomy and says
nothing about how notes are written.  Its characteristic move is
just-in-time reorganisation: things move between the four when you are
already there, and project start and end trigger sweeps.  Forte's areas
reuse Allen's Horizon 2 wording; the substantive difference is splitting
responsibility from interest.
- Primitives :: project; area; resource; archive; the project list, mirrored everywhere; the same four folders in every tool
- Rituals :: on filing: project, else area, else resource; move things when you are already there; project start: sweep for material; project end: extract reusable material, archive; setup: pair every project with a goal
- Rules :: organise by actionability, not subject; if it cannot end, it is an area; identical folders everywhere, shallow nesting; archive, never delete
- Cost :: Four choices at filing time, a project list kept in sync across tools by hand, kickoff and close sweeps, and the project-versus-area re-decision as things finish; the moves are the review and there are no tags.
- Distinct :: A single tool-agnostic partition with actionability as the only axis, and the hobby/dream test pairing every project with a goal.

*** [LYT] Linking Your Thinking (Nick Milo, 2020 kit; ACCESS 2022; ACE 2023)
Relieve the anxiety of a growing linked vault with "fluid frameworks": Maps
of Content and a Home note that curate access rather than limit it. Built
for Obsidian; it optimises for navigability and idea emergence from
colliding notes rather than finished writing.  Its characteristic move is
the MOC as a workbench you think in, created only at the "mental squeeze
point" when a topic's notes overwhelm, with folders by kind of note and
never by topic.  The folder scheme churned three times in three years, so it
is not load-bearing.
- Primitives :: note, atomised by necessity; Map of Content, MOC; Home note, MOC of MOCs; ACCESS folders by kind; ARC: add, relate, communicate; mental squeeze point
- Rituals :: add notes freely, a daily note in Calendar; at the squeeze point: gather, arrange, write connective text; use the topic's MOC as the workbench; return to Home to re-ground; no weekly cadence
- Rules :: "structure must be earned"; make a MOC at the squeeze point, not before; folders by kind, topics live in MOCs; split a note when its links go vague
- Cost :: MOCs are hand-curated link lists and every new note raises "which MOC?", though a stale MOC breaks nothing; kind-based folders make filing nearly automatic.
- Distinct :: The MOC as a workbench rather than an index, and the squeeze point as the trigger for adding structure.

*** [JD] Johnny.Decimal (Johnny Noble, 2015 site; in private use since 2010)
A fixed two-level numbered hierarchy so every thing has one short,
speakable, permanent address: at most ten areas, ten categories each, a
hundred IDs per category, nothing numbered deeper.  It optimises for
findability, memory and communication, numbers spoken out loud.  Its
characteristic move is index-first: the JDex entry is written before the
container exists, and reserved zeros give every level the same meta-slots
for inbox, tasks, templates and archive.  Born from a dance company's
Dropbox; it suits stable admin better than fluid interests.
- Primitives :: area, 10–19; category, 11, 12...; ID, 11.01, the key; standard zeros: .00 index, .01 inbox; JDex, one note per ID; multiple systems, SYS.AC.ID
- Rituals :: to file: ten, then ten, then the ID; to create an ID: write the JDex entry first; record locations and decisions in the JDex; drain each .01 inbox; archive into .09; no review cadence given
- Rules :: "no more than ten" at every level; never number deeper than the ID; numbers are permanent; do not mint a category for a one-off
- Cost :: An up-front design session and a hand-written JDex entry per ID, conceded "not free"; renumbering is forbidden so a poor design lingers, and pruning is per-category archiving.
- Distinct :: The address as primary key across file system, email and paper, with reserved zeros and an index that precedes the container.

*** [IR] Incremental reading and spaced repetition (Piotr Wozniak, 1999; Michael Nielsen, 2018; Anki; Readwise 2017, Reader 2022)
Treat what you have read as a queue that software schedules back to you.
Wozniak: read thousands of articles at once without getting lost; Nielsen: a
fact worth ten future minutes costs four to seven minutes of review over
twenty years.  Its characteristic move is the scheduler: review timing
computed from forgetting rather than from a calendar, with a priority queue
and auto-postpone so overload is handled by algorithm rather than guilt.
Readwise's Daily Review resurfaces highlights without authoring, at the
price of shallowness.
- Primitives :: extract, a scheduled mini-article; cloze deletion; priority, 0–100% per element; auto-postpone; scheduler, SM-2 or FSRS; Readwise Daily Review, Mastery mode; Reader inbox, later, archive
- Rituals :: daily review, 15–20 minutes; extract while reading, cloze on later passes; on overload: postpone, tune down, triage the inbox to zero; occasionally refactor bad cards; no weekly or yearly ritual
- Rules :: do not learn what you do not understand; minimum information per card; two or three related questions per topic, no yes/no; Anki "in service to some personal creative project"
- Cost :: The heaviest recurring obligation in its family, a daily session that grows with the deck and prompt authoring at 10–60 minutes per paper; pruning is built in through postpone, suspend, tune down and expiry.
- Distinct :: The only knowledge system with a scheduler, and overload handled by a priority queue rather than by the practitioner arriving.

*** [VOIT] Karl Voit's tagging philosophy (karl-voit.at; tagstore 2009–2012; "How to Use Tags" 2022)
Organise for retrieval, not filing: "you should always and only tag for your
future retrieval processes".  A folder gives an item one place; a few tags
from a small controlled vocabulary give it several, and hierarchies can be
generated from tags instead of stored.  Metadata lives in the file name.
Its characteristic move is the controlled vocabulary of about a dozen
documented tags, five to seven per file, under ten naming rules.  Its
retrieval claims rest on controlled experiments; the measured win was
acceptance, not speed.
- Primitives :: controlled vocabulary, about a dozen; tag: lowercase, plural, single word; file name: date, description, tags; TagTrees, generated hierarchies; mutually exclusive tag sets; one org category per file
- Rituals :: on creation: date prefix, description, vocabulary tags; retrieval by tag filter or TagTree, which is the review; occasional gardening of typos, unused and one-off tags
- Rules :: as few tags as possible; non-overlapping, general level, omit the obvious; "I force myself not to use too many tags"; if you use many tags, use full-text search instead
- Cost :: Per item a date, often derivable, a short description and up to seven tags from a fixed list; the vocabulary document is the one artefact needing care, and pruning means dropping unused tags or not tagging where search suffices.
- Distinct :: A concrete small vocabulary with naming rules carried in the file name, and hierarchy as a generated view over tags.

** Habits and behaviour design

*** [AH] Atomic Habits (James Clear, 2018)
System design in place of goal-setting: "you fall to the level of your
systems".  It optimises for consistency of small, identity-confirming
actions that compound, and moves the work from willpower to the shaping of
cues, friction and rewards.  Its characteristic move is the four-laws
matrix, make it obvious, attractive, easy, satisfying, with explicit
inversions for breaking a habit, applied through one-sentence implementation
intentions and habit stacks that bind a new behaviour to a trigger that
already exists.  A synthesis of Fogg, Duhigg, Gollwitzer and Milkman rather
than new evidence.
- Primitives :: habit loop: cue, craving, response, reward; four laws and inversions; implementation intention; habit stack; habits scorecard, +, –, =; habit tracker, a calendar; habit contract; identity-based habits
- Rituals :: in the moment: tick the tracker at once; once: the scorecard, one intention or stack per habit; daily: only the marks; yearly: Annual Review and a mid-year Integrity Report
- Rules :: two-minute rule, "read one page", distinct from Allen's; "never miss twice"; Goldilocks rule, tasks on the edge of ability; "what is immediately rewarded is repeated"
- Cost :: Front-loaded and low: one sentence per habit decided once, one mark per day for at most three or four habits, two short yearly essays; the scorecard prunes, and a habit is "a lifestyle to be lived, not a finish line".
- Distinct :: The four-laws matrix with inversions, the scorecard audit before adding anything, and an Integrity Report kept separate from the outcomes review.

*** [TH] Tiny Habits (BJ Fogg, 2019)
Behaviour is a design problem, not a character problem: "Behavior happens
when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt come together at the same moment."
Because motivation cannot be controlled, the method designs for ability and
prompt: shrink the behaviour to about thirty seconds, attach it to an
existing routine, feel success at once.  Its characteristic move is the
recipe, "After I [ANCHOR], I will [TINY BEHAVIOR]", followed by a deliberate
celebration, because "it's not repetition, it's the emotion" that wires the
habit.  Nothing is tracked.
- Primitives :: aspiration; swarm of Bs, twenty candidates; focus map, golden behaviours; recipe: anchor, tiny behaviour; celebration, "shine"; ability chain: time, money, effort, routine; motivation wave; pearl habit
- Rituals :: anchor ends, tiny behaviour, celebrate within seconds; let a behaviour grow only when it wants to; weekly: troubleshoot recipes, prompt first, ability second, motivation last; per project: Behavior Design's seven steps
- Rules :: help people do what they already want to do; help people feel successful; emotions create habits, not repetition; blame the design, never the person
- Cost :: Near zero: a few one-line recipes rewritten only when they fail, no streaks or tracking; a recipe that does not take is redesigned or dropped without judgement.
- Distinct :: B=MAP as a diagnostic with a strict troubleshooting order, and celebration as the mechanism.

*** [POH] The Power of Habit (Charles Duhigg, 2012)
A journalist's synthesis of habit neuroscience and organisational cases,
solving the diagnosis of an existing habit and its replacement on the
principle that "you can't extinguish a bad habit, you can only change it".
It optimises for keeping the cue and reward while swapping the routine, and
for picking the few keystone habits whose change spreads.  Its
characteristic move is the diagnostic schema: almost every cue is a
location, a time, an emotional state, other people or the immediately
preceding action, logged when the urge hits.
- Primitives :: habit loop: cue, routine, reward; five cue categories; keystone habit; small win; belief and group
- Rituals :: one-off diagnosis: identify the routine, experiment with rewards, isolate the cue, have a plan; log the five cue fields when the urge hits; a fifteen-minute test of whether the urge persists; daily: run the plan on the cue
- Rules :: golden rule: same cue, same reward, new routine; change keystone habits first; "there isn't one formula for changing habits, there are thousands"
- Cost :: A few days of a five-field urge log plus one written plan, nothing recurring; Duhigg admits the plan took weeks to become automatic.
- Distinct :: The five cue categories as a diagnostic schema, reward experimentation with a fifteen-minute test, and keystone habits as the selection heuristic.

*** [HT] Habit tracking and its research base (Seinfeld via Isaac, 2007; Lally 2010; Wood; Milkman; Silverman & Barasch 2023; Loop, Streaks, Habitica)
Not a system but a practice: record whether a behaviour happened and show
the record back.  The research says habits are context-triggered, take weeks
to months in a stable context, and are shaped by the display itself.  Lally
2010's "66 days" is the median for the 39 of 96 participants whose curves
fitted, range 18 to 254, and one missed day "did not materially affect"
formation.  Silverman and Barasch show an intact streak motivates and a
broken one demotivates regardless of behaviour, softened when repairable.
Sharif and Shu's emergency reserves and Loop's strength score, a 13-day
half-life, are the forgiving designs the evidence favours; Seinfeld disowned
the chain.
- Primitives :: chain, "don't break the chain"; streak, reset to zero; strength score, exponential smoothing; grid or calendar heat-map; gamified value, Habitica; emergency reserve, two skips weekly; temporal landmark, fresh start; temptation bundle
- Rituals :: one mark per habit, ideally from a sensor; daily glance at the display; weekly: the reserve resets, Monday is a fresh start
- Rules :: "never miss twice" as the readable version; rewards must land within about a minute (Wood); a repair option softens a broken streak; rigid clock-time incentives cut persistence (Beshears 2021)
- Cost :: One tick per habit per day, with the cost hiding in the display: chains demand perfection, rates and reserves do not, Habitica adds an economy and paper grids are redrawn monthly.
- Distinct :: Evidence that display design, not only behaviour, drives persistence, and the emergency reserve as slack with a small cost.

*** [IND] Indistractable (Nir Eyal, 2019)
Distraction is defined relative to intent: traction is any action that moves
you toward what you really want, and "you can't call something a distraction
unless you know what it is distracting you from".  It optimises for doing
what you said you would do, and measures exactly that. Its characteristic
move is a timeboxed calendar built from values across three life domains,
you, relationships, work, defended by pacts of effort, price and identity,
and reviewed weekly by asking when you did what you intended and when you
got distracted.
- Primitives :: internal triggers, discomfort; external triggers, pings and people; values and three life domains; timebox; pacts: effort, price, identity; schedule sync
- Rituals :: in the moment: notice the discomfort, write the trigger down, ten-minute rule; daily: follow the timeboxed calendar; weekly: fifteen minutes to reflect and refine the template; sync the schedule with stakeholders
- Rules :: precommitment removes the future choice; "time management is pain management"; batch email, require agendas, mute group chat; time limits defend against Parkinson's law
- Cost :: A weekly calendar template built once and refined fifteen minutes a week plus a trigger note whenever you slip; the metric, intention kept, is already recorded by the calendar, and a timebox forces the decision a rolled-forward to-do item avoids.
- Distinct :: The definitional move that distraction exists only relative to a plan, the three-pact taxonomy, and schedule syncing as a negotiated artefact.

*** [EM] Energy management (Loehr & Schwartz, 2003; Daniel Pink, When, 2018)
Two books that say the scarce resource is energy and timing, not hours.
Loehr and Schwartz: manage energy, not time, across four sources, physical,
emotional, mental, spiritual, oscillating between expenditure and recovery
on 90-to-120-minute rhythms, with rituals built once.  Pink: most days run
peak, trough, rebound, ordered by chronotype; analytic work at the peak,
admin in the trough, insight work in the rebound.  Their characteristic move
is time of day as a first-class scheduling input and recovery as a
deliverable rather than a gap.  The 90-minute figure is loosely borrowed.
- Primitives :: four energy sources; oscillation, ultradian rhythms; positive energy ritual; chronotype: lark, owl, third bird; peak, trough, rebound; nappuccino; restorative break rules
- Rituals :: a real break every 90–120 minutes; daily: analytic work at the peak, insight at the rebound, a nap in the trough; once: log energy for a week to find the trough; per change: define purpose, face the truth, install rituals
- Rules :: "full engagement requires drawing on four separate but related sources of energy"; balance expenditure with intermittent renewal; something beats nothing, moving beats stationary, outside beats inside; chronotype is a distribution, not a badge
- Cost :: A week of energy self-ratings once and rituals written once that are meant to disappear into routine; nothing daily.
- Distinct :: Time of day as a scheduling input and recovery treated as a deliverable.

** Notebooks, journaling, reflection and time units

*** [BUJO] The Bullet Journal Method (Ryder Carroll, 2013 site; 2018 book)
A paper system Carroll built to manage his own ADHD: capture must be fast
enough to survive a distracted mind and pruning must be forced, because
nothing else will do it.  It optimises for speed of entry, Rapid Logging in
one-glyph bullets, and deliberate re-decision, Migration; the medium is the
point, "analog on purpose".  Its characteristic move is migration as
pruning: every open task is rewritten by hand monthly, and "if an entry
isn't worth the effort to rewrite, then it's probably not that important.
Get rid of it."
- Primitives :: Rapid Logging; task •, event ○, note –; task states: × done, > migrated, < scheduled; signifiers * and !; index and threading; future, monthly and daily logs; collections
- Rituals :: rapid log in the moment; AM reflection: reread, date the page, plan; PM reflection: mark, strike, migrate, unload the head; monthly: set up the next log, migrate, review the future log; yearly: new notebook, re-migrate only surviving collections
- Rules :: "if an entry isn't worth the effort to rewrite ... get rid of it"; "migration is more than just rewriting your to-dos"; entries short and objective; no page before it is needed
- Cost :: High and intentional: every open task is rewritten by hand monthly, the index, threading and logs are drawn by hand, and a new notebook means rewriting the future log; the friction exists to force a re-decision on every item at a fixed cadence.
- Distinct :: Migration, rewriting as the pruning mechanism, and a one-glyph grammar that keeps tasks, events and notes in one stream with state carried in the symbol.

*** [IJ] Interstitial journaling (Tony Stubblebine, 2017)
One running journal with a timestamped line or two at every transition
between tasks: what you just did, what you are about to do, what is in the
way.  The target is the interstitial moment, where procrastination starts
and the previous task's loose ends leak into the next.  Stubblebine's
provocation is that it can replace the to-do list, since the next action is
written when you know it best.  Its characteristic move is putting the write
at the transition, so journal, task log and time log become one artefact.
- Primitives :: entry: clock time plus a sentence; the stream, one page per day; the transition, the unit
- Rituals :: before the next thing, write what you finished and what comes next; back from a break, name it; daily: read back for the timeline; weekly review optional
- Rules :: write at the switch, not during the task; name the next task's first step; name the break honestly; dump the previous task's open threads
- Cost :: Fifteen to twenty-five lines a day, seconds each, append-only and disposable; nothing is rewritten and there is no pruning because there is no state to keep current, which is also its weakness.
- Distinct :: The only system that writes at the transition rather than at capture or review, making the time log a by-product of thinking.

*** [DN] Daily notes as the universal inbox (Roam Research, 2019; Logseq, 2020; Obsidian daily-notes)
A page per date, created and opened by default, that receives everything:
tasks, meeting notes, ideas, reading.  Filing happens afterwards and
automatically, since a topic page shows in its linked references every dated
block that mentioned it; the daily page is inbox and journal at once and
topic pages are compiled views.  It optimises for zero filing decisions at
capture: "this removes the question: 'where should I store this?'"  Its
characteristic move is backlinks compiling the index nobody writes, with
block references reusing one fact in many contexts without copies.
- Primitives :: daily page, dated; block, the atomic unit; page link, =[[Topic]]=; block reference and embed; linked references, the compiled index; template; query, a saved search
- Rituals :: type into today's page, bracket names as you go; the page opens on launch; occasionally promote a grown topic to its own page; no migration is mandated
- Rules :: "it really doesn't matter where you store your notes ... as long as you write in outlines and link them"; link nouns liberally, the link is the filing; promote to a page only when a topic has grown; tasks stay where born, surfaced by query
- Cost :: Near zero at capture, moving to linking and one-off template and query setup; nothing is rewritten and nothing prunes, so journals accumulate forever and a March TODO is invisible unless a query asks.
- Distinct :: Backlinks compile the index automatically, and block references give one fact many contexts with no copies.

*** [REV] Review cadences (Allen; Forte; Newport; Sacha Chua; Tim Ferriss; YearCompass; James Clear; CGP Grey & Myke Hurley's Theme System)
The review turns a capture habit into a trusted system: at a fixed interval
the person empties inputs, reconciles lists with reality and re-chooses; the
interval sets what is re-chosen, actions and calendar weekly, projects
quarterly, direction yearly.  Allen's weekly review is eleven steps; Forte's
is five inboxes in under thirty minutes; Newport's weekly plan matches work
to the time actually available; Chua's is a narrative and a time table
generated from Org.  The annual reviewers, Ferriss, YearCompass and Clear,
share a move the others lack: derive next year from last year's evidence.
Grey and Hurley's Theme System replaces the goal with a direction that
"can't fail".
- Primitives :: Allen's eleven-step checklist; Forte's five-inbox list; Newport's weekly narrative; Chua's generated time table; Ferriss's POSITIVE and NEGATIVE columns; YearCompass booklet, past then ahead; Clear's three questions; yearly theme, daily circles
- Rituals :: daily theme circles; weekly: Allen, Forte on Sunday, Newport on Monday, Chua published; quarterly: Newport's seasonal plan; yearly: Ferriss's calendar walk, YearCompass, Clear's questions
- Rules :: every project has at least one next action; personalise the checklist until sustainable; review the calendar, not your memory; "a good theme can't fail"
- Cost :: The weekly review is the cost, thirty minutes to two hours re-deciding every open project, and annual reviews are cheap only if an archive exists to walk; Chua's is nearly free because it is generated from data she already keeps.
- Distinct :: Ferriss's evidence-first annual review whose outputs are bookings and a not-to-do list, the Theme System's direction instead of goals, and Chua's review as a report generated from the system's own logs.

*** [MT] Make Time (Jake Knapp & John Zeratsky, 2018)
A four-step daily loop, Highlight, Laser, Energize, Reflect, for one person
who wants one meaningful thing a day to survive the Busy Bandwagon and the
Infinity Pools.  It optimises for a single 60-to-90-minute Highlight chosen
by urgency, satisfaction or joy, protected by a calendar block, and judged
by whether it happened rather than by task count.  Its characteristic move
is the nightly Reflect note as an experiment log: which of 87 numbered
tactics you tried, whether it worked, and focus and energy each scored one
to ten.
- Primitives :: Highlight, 60–90 minutes; Might-Do list; "do not schedule" calendar block; tactics, 87 numbered experiments; Reflect note, six fields
- Rituals :: morning or the night before: choose and block the Highlight; during: Laser and Energize tactics; evening: the Reflect note; no weekly ritual, the loop repeats daily
- Rules :: one Highlight a day, medium-sized; rotate urgency, satisfaction and joy; treat each day as an experiment; judge the day by the Highlight, not the task count
- Cost :: Minimal by design, one line for the Highlight and a six-field note at night; the might-do list is never groomed and pruning is by neglect.
- Distinct :: The Reflect note's experiment framing, daily numeric self-report of focus and energy, and joy as a legitimate criterion for the day's priority.

*** [POM] The Pomodoro Technique (Francesco Cirillo, late 1980s; paper 2006; book 2018)
A 25-minute atomic unit of effort plus a paper process around it, aiming to
alleviate anxiety about "becoming", cut interruptions, raise awareness of
decisions and refine estimation.  It optimises for measured effort; the
timer is only the starting point.  Its characteristic move is the estimation
loop: estimate each activity in pomodoros, mark each one that rings, record
estimate, actual and difference daily, treat a second or third estimate as a
quality signal, and set tomorrow's "available pomodoros" from today's count.
Most adopters keep the timer and drop the sheets.
- Primitives :: pomodoro, 25 minutes, indivisible; Activity Inventory Sheet; To Do Today Sheet, available pomodoros; Records Sheet: estimate, real, diff; marks: X, apostrophe, dash; Unplanned & Urgent section; five stages, planning to visualizing
- Rituals :: start of day: choose, estimate, fill only the available pomodoros; each pomodoro: timer, X on the ring, short break; after four, a long break; on interruption: mark it, then inform, negotiate, call back; end of day: record, process, visualize
- Rules :: "if a Pomodoro begins, it has to ring"; "the Pomodoro is indivisible"; more than 5–7 pomodoros, break it down; less than one, add it up
- Cost :: Far heavier than the timer suggests: three sheets, a daily list hand-copied from the inventory to re-decide, a mark per pomodoro and interruption, and a nightly transcription of estimate against actual; purposeless activities are deleted from the inventory.
- Distinct :: A cheap estimation feedback loop with second and third estimates as a quality signal, a two-glyph interruption taxonomy, and daily capacity read from yesterday's count.

*** [TBX] Timeboxing (Marc Zao-Sanders, HBR 2018, book 2024; Nir Eyal, 2019)
"Migrating to-do lists into calendars": every task gets a start and an end,
and the calendar becomes both plan and record.  Zao-Sanders ranks it first
of 100 hacks and keeps three box sizes, 15, 30 and 60 minutes, so sizing
takes no thought. Eyal's version is values-first, building the week from
three life domains.  Its characteristic move is plan and log as one object:
what gets no box does not happen, because the week has a fixed number of
hours, and last week's corrected calendar is the record.
- Primitives :: box: one task, a start, an end; three sizes: 15, 30, 60; calendar as single source of truth; what, when, one, enough; Eyal's weekly template by domain; Eyal's two weekly questions
- Rituals :: daily: fifteen minutes turning the list into boxes; in the box: one thing, then stop or re-box; weekly: fifteen minutes classifying each miss as internal trigger, external trigger or planning problem; correct boxes when reality diverges
- Rules :: a start and an end time for every task; Parkinson's law as a tool; "good enough" is done; "think like a scientist, not a drill sergeant"
- Cost :: Each task entered twice unless the list is dropped, boxes re-dragged when the day diverges, and Eyal's template rebuilt weekly; pruning is automatic and brutal, since what gets no box does not exist.
- Distinct :: Plan and log as one object with no separate tracking step, and the only system here that claims an empirical ranking for itself.

*** [TA] Time audits and time tracking (Laura Vanderkam, 168 Hours, 2010; Toggl-style timers; Parkinson, 1955)
Track a whole week, all 168 hours, in half-hour cells, then read it: "how
can you spend your time better if you don't know how you're spending it
now?"  The week, not the day, is the unit because "there are no typical
weeks".  One claim underlies the practice: tracked time is the only honest
basis for estimates and capacity.  Parkinson's law, "work expands so as to
fill the time available", is why deadlines and boxes are set tighter than
the estimate.  Its characteristic move is the week as the budget.
- Primitives :: time log, seven columns from 5 a.m.; entry, free text at any grain; categories: work, sleep, exercise...; timer log with project tags; Parkinson deadline, below natural duration
- Rituals :: check in three or four times a day, a minute each, back-filling; weekly: finish the 168 hours, categorise, ask what to do more and less of; calibration: compare actuals to estimates and plans
- Rules :: track a week, not a day; write what you did, not what you meant; "I don't have time" means "it's not a priority"; an estimate without a log behind it is a guess
- Cost :: A few minutes a day for one challenge week, or a click per switch plus a category decision with timers, where the hidden cost is taxonomy upkeep; the log is disposable after analysis and pruning applies to the category list.
- Distinct :: The 168-hour frame, honest actuals as the calibration source for every other system's estimates, and Parkinson's law used deliberately.

** Constraint, focus and prioritisation

*** [PK] Personal Kanban (Jim Benson & Tonianne DeMaria Barry, 2011)
Toyota's kanban applied to one person's knowledge work, where the inventory
is ideas, emails, requests and projects and the problem is invisible,
unbounded work and the "existential overhead" of half-finished tasks.  It
optimises for flow and completion, not for capturing everything. "Personal
Kanban has only two rules: visualize your work and limit WIP".  Its
characteristic move is the WIP limit enforced at the moment of starting, and
the backlog reframed as Options, so pulling a card into Ready is the act of
commitment.
- Primitives :: board: Options, Ready, Doing, Done; card; WIP limit, two or three; the pen for blocked work, name unverified; Today column; retrospective column; explicit policies
- Rituals :: pull, never push; when Doing is full, finish or demote; optionally fill Today each morning; weekly retrospective fed by Done; adjust the WIP limit and columns from the retro
- Rules :: two rules, everything else decoration; start with an arbitrary WIP limit and tune it; small tasks are "a poor man's WIP limit"; "pull when you can, be pushed when you must"
- Cost :: One card per item and the act of moving it, with no daily re-writing; the backlog is guilt-free options and the WIP limit forces demotion, but no backlog-pruning cadence is prescribed and the weekly retro has to carry it.
- Distinct :: The WIP limit as a hard, always-on constraint at the moment of starting, and option versus commitment as column semantics.

*** [MF] Mark Forster's systems: Do It Tomorrow, Autofocus, Final Version and FVP (2006; 2009; 2012–2015)
Three successive answers to the open to-do list.  Do It Tomorrow answers
with closed lists: everything arriving today goes on tomorrow's will-do
list, sized to a day, plus one "current initiative" touched first every
morning.  Autofocus answers with one long catch-all list processed by
intuition, "standing out", where a page passed with nothing chosen is
dismissed, so pruning is a side effect of processing.  Final Version and FVP
answer with a questioning chain, "what do I want to do more than X?",
ordering work by psychological readiness.  Forster's constant: "the question
is not what priority something is, but whether it needs doing at all."
- Primitives :: closed list, the will-do list; mañana principle; current initiative, one project; the long list, pages of 25–35; standing out; re-entry at the end; dismissal, highlighted not deleted; FVP dot chain and root task
- Rituals :: DIT: write tomorrow's list, initiative first, mañana everything new; Autofocus: scan a page until an item stands out, work "as long as you feel like", re-enter the unfinished; dismiss a page passed cold; FVP: dot the chain, work from the last dot, re-scan the tail; periodic review of dismissed items; "little and often"
- Rules :: "don't prioritise your to-do list"; urgency "relates to when the task needs to be started"; dismissal is "do it NOW, or admit that you are not going to do it at all"; "the best way to sink any time management system is to overload it right at the beginning"
- Cost :: Entry costs nothing; the cost is re-writing half to two-thirds of touched items on paper plus dating, highlighting and page marks, which Forster calls a feature; pruning is native through dismissal, weeding and the DIT audit that cuts commitments, and he opposed Someday/Maybe lists outright.
- Distinct :: Intuition as the selection algorithm, dismissal as pruning by processing rather than by review, and the FVP chain that orders work by pairwise "want" without ranking the whole list.

*** [AR] Agile Results (J.D. Meier, 2010)
A weekly and daily results loop: three outcomes at a time, outcomes not
activities, and a Friday learning loop.  It optimises for a fresh start and
momentum rather than completeness, "if you fall off the horse, you can get
back on".  Its characteristic move is the nested Rule of 3, three wins for
today, this week, this month, this year, chosen on Monday by asking "if this
were Friday, what three things would I want under my belt?", with hot spots
carrying minimum and maximum boundaries per life area.
- Primitives :: Three Wins, the Rule of 3; hot spots: mind, body, career...; scannable outcomes per hot spot; weekly and daily outcomes; triage: do, queue, schedule, delegate; 30-day improvement sprints
- Rituals :: Monday Vision: three wins for the week; Daily Outcomes: three wins, said aloud; Friday Reflection: three going well, three to improve; monthly and yearly three wins
- Rules :: Rule of 3; fresh start, the undone is not carried unless re-chosen; "fix time, flex scope"; worst things first, play to strengths
- Cost :: Three lines a day, three a week, six on Friday, with hot-spot lists kept loosely; nothing tracks the undone, so pruning is not-carrying-forward, a quiet dismissal without Forster's review questions.
- Distinct :: The nested Rule of 3 across horizons, Friday Reflection as a fixed pair of questions, and hot spots with minimum and maximum boundaries as a portfolio view.

*** [R3] Chris Bailey's Rule of 3, The Productivity Project and Hyperfocus (2016; 2018)
A year of self-experiments distilled into "productivity is how well you
manage your time, attention, and energy".  The Rule of 3 is borrowed from
Meier; the additions are measuring energy rather than assuming it and
batching low-return work into a maintenance day.  Hyperfocus adds attention
management, with deliberate wandering, scatterfocus, as a mode. Its
characteristic move is biological prime time: log energy hourly for three
weeks without caffeine or alarms, then put the highest-leverage work into
the measured peaks.  An n-of-one experiment presented as findings.
- Primitives :: three intentions: today, week, year; time, attention, energy triad; biological prime time; maintenance day; four task types; hyperfocus and scatterfocus; accomplishments list
- Rituals :: morning: fast-forward to the end of the day, name three; weekly and yearly, the same question; a one-off three-week energy log; weekly maintenance day for chores; Sunday review of the accomplishments list
- Rules :: "we're wired to think in threes"; "at first, you'll dramatically over and underestimate how much capacity you have"; the 20-second rule for distractions; work fewer hours
- Cost :: Six lines a day and week, a one-off log of about fifteen entries a day for three weeks, and a maintenance day that removes bookkeeping by batching; unchosen things are not tracked, so pruning is implicit.
- Distinct :: Energy measured rather than assumed, the maintenance day as batching of low-return work, and the time, attention, energy triad as a taxonomy for tactics.

*** [ESS] Essentialism (Greg McKeown, 2014)
"Less, but better": "it's not about getting less done, it's about getting
only the right things done."  It optimises for the highest possible
contribution by making trade-offs explicit and refusing the many merely good
options; a selection discipline, not a list system, in three parts, explore,
eliminate, execute.  Its characteristic move is the 90 percent rule: score
an option 0 to 100 on its single most important criterion and "if you rate
it any lower than 90 percent, then automatically change the rating to 0 and
simply reject it."
- Primitives :: essential intent; three core truths; explore, eliminate, execute; the 90 percent rule; buffer, add 50 percent; "protect the asset", sleep; uncommit tools: zero-based budgeting, reverse pilot; trade-off questions
- Rituals :: scheduled time to think, read, sleep, play; the slow yes and the quick no; periodic zero-based review of commitments; reverse pilots; buffers on every estimate; no fixed cadence in the book
- Rules :: "if it isn't a clear yes, then it's a clear no"; the 90 percent rule; "which problem do I want?"; "a clear 'no' can be more graceful than a vague 'yes'"
- Cost :: Almost none on paper: one essential intent, a filter at intake and uncommitment, a score at decision time; pruning is a third of the method and nothing is stored for later, the endowment-effect argument being against keeping options.
- Distinct :: A numeric selectivity threshold applied at intake, uncommitment as a toolkit, and trade-offs framed as choosing a problem.

*** [ONE] The ONE Thing (Gary Keller & Jay Papasan, 2013)
Extraordinary results come from narrowing, not widening.  The Focusing
Question, "What's the ONE Thing I can do such that by doing it everything
else will be easier or unnecessary?", is asked at every horizon and its
answer protected by a time block of no less than four hours.  It optimises
for leverage, not coverage.  Its characteristic move is goal setting to the
now: someday, five-year, one-year, month, week, today, right now, each rung
"based on" the one above.  The 66-day figure is Lally's mean, whose range
ran 18 to 254.
- Primitives :: Focusing Question, big and small; goal ladder, seven rungs; time block, four hours; block order: time off, ONE Thing, planning; domino effect; six lies; four thieves; success list versus to-do list
- Rituals :: right now: answer the small-focus question, then do it; daily: the ONE Thing early, "if you erase, you must replace"; a weekly hour reviewing yearly and monthly goals; monthly goal from the yearly; year-end review of someday and five-year goals; name three interruptions and counters in advance
- Rules :: the Focusing Question; "start with the big stuff and see where it takes you"; start with 30 minutes and build; say no to everything that is not the ONE Thing
- Cost :: A seven-rung ladder written once and re-derived when goals move, one block a day, one hour a week, a year-end review; pruning is total, the method refuses to maintain a list of many things.
- Distinct :: The leverage question as selection criterion, the seven-rung ladder tying "right now" to "someday", and the block order with time off first.

*** [4KW] Four Thousand Weeks (Oliver Burkeman, 2021)
"If you live to be 80, you'll have had about 4,000 weeks."  Productivity
advice that promises to get everything done makes things worse, so "the
central challenge of time management isn't becoming more efficient, but
deciding what to neglect".  It optimises for meaningful commitment under
finitude.  Its characteristic move is the fixed-volume approach: an open
list of everything, a closed list of ten or fewer, three in motion, and
deciding in advance what to fail at.  The Buffett 25/5 story is, by his own
hedge, probably apocryphal.
- Primitives :: open list; closed list, ten or fewer; work-in-progress limit of three; predetermined time boundaries; done list, empty each morning; middling priorities, the 25/5 story; strategic underachievement; "pay yourself first"
- Rituals :: no new item onto the closed list until one leaves; daily: pay yourself first, fill the done list; when overloaded: serialise, tolerate the anxiety; periodically nominate what to fail at; no weekly review prescribed
- Rules :: "adopt a 'fixed volume' approach"; "serialize, serialize, serialize"; "decide in advance what to fail at"; "focus on what you've already completed"
- Cost :: Two lists and a daily done list, nothing rewritten; the open list is allowed to be a mess and mostly ignored, so pruning is by neglect and failure is chosen in advance rather than discovered in review.
- Distinct :: The efficiency trap as an argument against optimisation as such, and the critique of systems as anxiety management.

*** [12WY] The 12 Week Year (Brian Moran & Michael Lennington, 2013)
Replace the annual goal with a twelve-week "year" so that every week counts,
on the diagnosis that people "don't fail because they lack goals, they fail
because they lack execution".  It optimises for execution percentage and
urgency.  Its characteristic move is the weekly scorecard: one to three
goals per cycle, tactics assigned to weeks, and the share of this week's
tactics completed reported to a peer accountability meeting; the book's
threshold is 85 percent, the company's web copy 80.  Execution percent
measures activity, which Agile Results and OKRs reject.
- Primitives :: vision; 12-week goals, one to three; tactics assigned to weeks; weekly scorecard, execution score; lead versus lag indicators; Weekly Accountability Meeting; performance time: strategic, buffer, breakout blocks; the 13th week
- Rituals :: quarterly: vision to plan; weekly: plan from the 12-week plan, score last week, WAM; daily: work the plan, buffer blocks; one three-hour strategic block weekly; 13th-week review
- Rules :: 80–85 percent execution "most likely achieves goals"; keep score; one to three goals; intentional imbalance
- Cost :: High and openly so: a tactic-by-week plan, a weekly plan, a weekly tally, meeting attendance and calendar blocks, re-derived every twelve weeks and every week; pruning comes from the goal cap and from tactics that roll forward dragging the score down.
- Distinct :: The execution score as a weekly lead metric, the compressed year as an urgency device, and the three block types.

*** [OKR] Personal OKRs (Andy Grove, 1970s; John Doerr, 1999 and 2018; adapted to individuals)
"I will (Objective) as measured by (Key Results)": an objective that is
concrete and inspirational, three to five key results that are specific,
time-bound and aggressive, on a quarterly cycle, graded 0 to 1 where 0.7 on
a stretch goal means it was bold enough.  It optimises for alignment and
honest measurement; the personal adaptation keeps the measurement and loses
the alignment.  Gothelf calls individual OKRs an anti-pattern that devolves
into task lists ("read 5 books"); Wodtke's variant sets one objective and
three results at five-in-ten confidence, re-graded weekly.
- Primitives :: objective; key results, three to five; quarterly cycle; committed versus aspirational; grade 0.0–1.0, colour bands; Wodtke's weekly confidence
- Rituals :: quarterly set; weekly confidence check-in; end-of-cycle grade with reflection; do not change OKRs mid-cycle
- Rules :: three to five key results; aim for 0.7 on stretch goals; outcomes not outputs; don't tie grades to performance review
- Cost :: One objective and three to five numbers a quarter, updated weekly, but every key result needs a measurable data source or it silently becomes an activity; pruning is the cap on key results.
- Distinct :: Explicit stretch calibration at 0.7 with a committed/aspirational split, and weekly confidence as a leading signal.

*** [FTVS] Fixed time, variable scope (Shape Up's appetite, 2019; Parkinson's law, 1955; Andreessen's card and the done list, 2007)
Fix the time and let the scope flex.  Parkinson: "work expands so as to fill
the time available for its completion".  Shape Up: "an appetite is
completely different from an estimate ... appetites start with a number and
end with a design", with a circuit breaker that refuses extension and no
backlog, since "really important ideas will come back to you".  Andreessen:
a 3×5 card of tomorrow's three to five things, its back an Anti-Todo list
torn up at day's end.  Its characteristic move is refusing to keep a
backlog.
- Primitives :: appetite: small batch, big batch; bet and six-week cycle; circuit breaker; scope hammering, "~" nice-to-haves; no backlog; Andreessen's Todo, Watch, Later; the daily card; Anti-Todo or done list
- Rituals :: per cycle: set the appetite, bet, build, stop at the breaker, cool down; per day: write the card at night, fill the back, "marvel", tear it up
- Rules :: "fixed time, variable scope"; "scope grows like grass"; "cutting scope isn't lowering quality"; "if the idea is important, it'll come back"
- Cost :: An appetite and a bet per cycle with no backlog grooming, and a disposable daily card with zero retention; pruning is the absence of a place to keep things.
- Distinct :: The refusal to keep a backlog at all, the disposable daily card, and appetite as a design constraint rather than a forecast.


* Part 2 — The overlap: crosswalk, convergent core, tensions, composite

This is the bulk of the document.  2.1 takes each concept that recurs
across Part 1 and lists every system's version of it; 2.2 ranks the
concepts by how many systems arrive at them; 2.3 states the real
disagreements and resolves each one for this setup; 2.4 states the
composite in full.  Where a resolution leans on what already exists in
the config, it says so and Part 3 picks it up.


** 2.1 The concept crosswalk

*** How to read this
Codes are the bracketed ones from Part 1.  "Convergence: N" counts the
systems whose brief names the concept as a working part, not systems that
merely would not object.  Lineage copies (big rocks: FTF -> ZTD -> Newport)
are counted here and then discounted in 2.2, where it matters.  "The
inventory" is Appendix B: the config as it stood on 2026-09-09.

*** The concepts, one entry each

**** Universal capture  (convergence: 17)
One trusted, always-available place where anything with your attention is
written down at once, raw, with no decision beyond "worth a jot".
- GTD :: "In"; as few buckets as possible; a complete inventory, Someday included.
- ZTD :: the always-carried notebook; then halve the list.
- TBP :: collection columns on the day's page, so capture never leaves the block.
- MSP/WWE :: "jot down every loop that opens" into a plain text file; "To process".
- ZK :: the fleeting note, rewritten or discarded within a day or two.
- EN :: the daily log and the writing inbox; "zero friction".
- BASB :: capture what resonates: inspiring, useful, personal, surprising.
- JD :: a .01 inbox at every level.
- IR :: the Reader inbox; triaged, not cleared.
- BUJO :: rapid logging: one objective line, one glyph.
- IJ :: a timestamped line at every transition.
- DN :: today's page receives everything; filing is a backlink.
- POM :: the Activity Inventory; "U" for unplanned arrivals.
- MF :: Autofocus: "enter everything without trying to evaluate"; DIT: mañana.
- PK :: the backlog/options column.
- 4KW :: the open list, allowed to be a mess.
- FTVS :: Andreessen's Todo/Watch/Later; Basecamp refuses ("important ideas come back").
Agreement and divergence: every system that has an inbox agrees that
capture must be instant and decision-free, and that the head is the wrong
store.  They split on breadth: GTD wants the full inventory, ZTD halves it,
Forte filters by resonance, Forster catches everything but keeps nothing
that never stands out ("catch-all is about catching, not keeping"), and
Basecamp, Lee, Tracy, Keller and McKeown keep no inbox at all.  The
Newport line is that capture is right and list-driven execution is wrong.
Best formulation: GTD, because it defines the inbox as a promise to
process, which is what makes the head let go; Forster's "catch is not
keep" is the necessary amendment.
Evidence: none beyond practitioner report.
Decided by the person: whether the thought is worth a jot (a second).
Derivable: timestamp, source backlink, kind, routing, everything else.
The inventory already does this: n/N/t/m templates, :CREATED:, %a.

**** Capture and clarify are two motions  (convergence: 11)
The write is raw; the decision about what it is, whether it is a
commitment, and where it lives happens later, in a separate sitting.
- GTD :: the clarify flowchart: actionable? two minutes? delegate? defer?
- ZTD :: process inboxes daily; trash, delegate, file, schedule.
- BASB :: "no filing or tagging" at capture; organise by actionability later.
- EN :: the morning drain of the writing inbox is "the majority of my writing time".
- ZK :: fleeting note today, Zettel tomorrow.
- BUJO :: rapid log now; PM reflection and monthly migration decide.
- DN :: "removes the question: where should I store this?"; promote later.
- TBP :: collection columns emptied into the real system at shutdown.
- WWE :: "To process" column for the important-but-ambiguous.
- MF :: "the system itself will do the evaluation" on the scanning pass.
- PK :: pulling into Ready is the commitment; the backlog carries none.
Agreement and divergence: all agree that deciding at capture is what
makes capture stop.  They differ on who clarifies: a checklist (GTD), a
daily rewrite (ZK, EN), a review cadence (BuJo, Forte), a column move
(Kanban, Newport), or intuition on a later pass (Forster).  The two
systems without a clarify step, IJ and DN, have the worst stale-task
problem in the survey, which is the strongest argument for the split.
Best formulation: GTD, because the flowchart is a runnable algorithm;
Benson's amendment that the only decision that matters is option versus
commitment is what keeps it short.
Evidence: none beyond practitioner report.
Decided by the person: is this a commitment, and does it live somewhere
that already exists.
Derivable: kind (task / note / question / event / reference), the
two-minute class, project or area membership against known lists,
context, effort, dates in the text, related material.  This is the
decoration job the requirements name.

**** Next action, and the project as an outcome  (convergence: 14)
The unit of execution is a concrete, startable step; a project is an
outcome needing more than one, and the invariant is that every live
project has a next step or is declared dormant.
- GTD :: "the next physical, visible activity"; every project has one; a project ends within a year.
- ZTD :: context lists of next actions; projects list optional.
- FROG :: salami-slice or Swiss-cheese the task you cannot start.
- MSP :: projects are never decomposed into tasks; they get time directly; full-horizon rule.
- SP :: a project is anything needing more than one session; one project per deep day.
- WWE :: the card holds the whole conversation about one obligation.
- PARA :: a project has a goal and an end; "a project without a goal is a hobby".
- BASB :: intermediate packets; dial down the scope to ship.
- BUJO :: the task bullet, sub-tasks nested.
- MF :: "write outline for Project X report", not "write report"; little and often.
- PK :: a card is a task or a project; small tasks are "a poor man's WIP limit".
- AR/OKR :: outcomes, not activities; an activity-shaped win is a smell.
- 12WY :: tactics assigned to weeks.
- ONE :: "What's my ONE Thing right now?"
Agreement and divergence: everyone wants the unit small enough to start.
The real split is Newport against Allen: Allen decomposes every project
to its next action and lives off the lists; Newport keeps projects whole,
gives them calendar hours, and lets tasks live only on the boards.
Meier and Doerr want the unit phrased as an outcome, which pulls the
other way from "physical, visible".  Forster's "little and often" is the
reconciliation in practice: touch the project in a short session, and the
next action is whatever you write down when you stop.
Best formulation: GTD for the atomic unit and the machine-checkable
invariant; Newport's caveat for deep projects, which receive blocks, not
lists.
Evidence: none beyond practitioner report.
Decided by the person: what the next step is when a project is touched
(the Hemingway bridge sentence at the end of a session).
Derivable: projects with no open child, orphan actions, verb-shaped
titles, membership from shared nouns and links.  The inventory's Projects
view groups by parent and names "Standalone"; the invariant itself is not
yet checked.

**** Areas and roles, against resources  (convergence: 13)
An ongoing standard you are responsible for, with no end, distinguished
from a topic you are merely interested in; the axis along which balance is
judged and containers are named.
- GTD :: Horizon 2, areas of focus and accountability; checked monthly.
- FTF :: roles, about seven, one or two big rocks per role per week; a renewal row.
- PARA :: areas (responsible for) against resources (interested in); Allen's wording.
- BASB :: the monthly review edits the area list.
- AR :: hot spots (mind, body, emotions, career, financial, relationships, fun) with min/max boundaries.
- R3 :: a parallel personal three, for balance.
- IND :: three domains: you, relationships, work, in that order.
- SP/MSP :: missions, two or three; a strategic plan per role.
- WWE :: one board per role.
- DM :: leisure planned as its own area, seasonally.
- LYT :: Spaces, the big life areas, as a folder of kind.
- JD :: areas 10-19 ... 90-99, at most ten.
- REV :: YearCompass's life-areas grid; Clear's categories.
Agreement and divergence: Covey and Meier make the area the planning
axis (something per role per week, minima and maxima); Forte and Noble
make it a container; Newport limits how many you may hold at once.  The
PARA/GTD quarrel is small: Forte's "standard to be maintained" is Allen's
Horizon 2, and the substantive difference is splitting responsibility
from interest.  Forte's own failure case, an area masquerading as a
project ("Hiring"), is the thing to watch.
Best formulation: PARA's responsibility/interest split with Meier's
min/max boundaries, because the boundary is what makes an area reviewable
without a goal.
Evidence: none beyond practitioner report.
Decided by the person: which areas exist, and their minima and maxima.
Derivable: area membership of an item (classification), time per area
from the state log, areas with no activity for N weeks, an area with no open
item at all.  The inventory's (todo) files and #+CATEGORY are the areas
already; the notes directory is the resources.

**** Context and energy as the first filter  (convergence: 9)
What you can do right now is filtered first by where you are, what tools
you have, how much time there is and what state you are in; priority
comes after.
- GTD :: the four criteria in order: context, time, energy, priority.
- ZTD :: @work @personal @errands @calls as master lists.
- MF :: separate lists per location; "standing out" is felt readiness.
- DW :: deep versus shallow; the depth score; a shallow budget of 30-50%.
- TBP :: nothing under 30 minutes gets its own block; batch the shallow.
- SP :: admin outside the deep blocks; a slow season shrinks the day's goal.
- EM :: peak for analytic, rebound for insight, trough for admin; a break every 90-120 minutes.
- R3 :: biological prime time, measured hourly for three weeks.
- VOIT :: @Person tags as the context a meeting needs.
Agreement and divergence: Allen's contexts are places and tools; Newport's
is depth; Pink's is time of day; Forster's is a feeling.  They agree only
that the filter precedes the ranking.  Beshears (2021) cuts across the
habit side: binding a behaviour to a rigid clock hour reduced persistence
versus flexibility, so the context that matters is the anchor, not the
hour.
Best formulation: GTD's ordering with Pink's placement, because the four
criteria say when to ask about energy and Pink says what to do with the
answer.
Evidence: the Danish test-score study (afternoon exams scored as if two
weeks of school were missed; a 20-30 minute break more than repaired it);
the parole-judge study, effect size later disputed; Bailey's n=1.
Decided by the person: the energy tag, if any (@fresh/@tired in the
inventory), and where the routine puts the deep block.
Derivable: context from the verb and object, depth from the text, time of
day from the routine template, the fit of a task to the block at hand.

**** The daily focus set  (convergence: 17)
Before the day starts, a person chooses one to three things that will make
the day a success, and the day is judged by them, not by the count.
- ZTD :: one to three MITs, at least one advancing a goal; first, before email.
- FROG :: A-1, the frog, eaten first; "if two frogs, the ugliest first".
- IVY :: six, ranked, worked strictly in order; unfinished ones re-compete.
- FTF :: the day adapts the week's big rocks ("30/10").
- MT :: the Highlight, 60-90 minutes, chosen by urgency, satisfaction or joy.
- AR :: three wins for today, said aloud.
- R3 :: three intentions for today, plus a personal three.
- ONE :: the ONE Thing for today, blocked early.
- 4KW :: no more than three in progress; pay yourself first.
- BUJO :: the * priority signifier; the AM reflection.
- MSP/TBP :: the week's two or three big rocks placed as the day's first blocks.
- SP :: one project for the day's deep work.
- MF :: DIT's will-do list, sized to a day; the current initiative first thing.
- PK :: the optional Today column, pulled each morning.
- EIS :: Q1, "do first".
- FTVS :: Andreessen's card: three to five, written the night before.
- IND :: traction is "did I do what I intended".
Agreement and divergence: the shape is universal, one protected pick or a
tiny set; the rule for picking is not.  Hardest (Tracy), most important
(Babauta, Lee), most joyful (Knapp), highest leverage (Keller), felt
readiness (Forster), a rock per role (Covey).  The count is asserted from
different premises and defended by none.  Lineage inflates the number:
big rocks travel Covey -> Babauta -> Newport; MIT -> Meier's rule of three
-> Bailey.
Best formulation: Make Time's rotating criterion under Burkeman's cap of
three, because the rotation stops the frog from always winning and the
cap stops the set from becoming the list.
Evidence: none beyond practitioner report.
Decided by the person: the pick, irreducibly; every system, including
Forster's, leaves this to the human.  The inventory's NEXT state is this
decision made visible to the planner.
Derivable: ranked candidates, fit to capacity, block placement, the
count of days a candidate has been passed over.

**** Time is allocated, not listed  (convergence: 14)
Work gets a start and an end on the calendar; the list is consulted when
the day is planned and not during it; the plan is a hypothesis to revise.
- TBP :: "give every minute a job"; revision columns; no bonus for sticking to the plan.
- MSP :: the weekly plan matches work to the time actually available.
- DW :: 4-7 hour deep blocks booked four weeks ahead like medical appointments.
- ONE :: block the ONE Thing early, "no less than four hours"; "if you erase, you must replace".
- FTF/ZTD :: big rocks booked into the calendar first, then gravel.
- TBX :: the calendar as single source of truth; three sizes, 15/30/60.
- IND :: timeboxes derived from values; "think like a scientist".
- POM :: the To Do Today sheet sized to "Available Pomodoros".
- 12WY :: strategic, buffer and breakout blocks.
- MT :: a "do not schedule" block for the Highlight.
- AH :: "I will [BEHAVIOR] at [TIME] in [LOCATION]" is a block.
- EIS :: Q2 gets a date.
- DM :: leisure blocks from a seasonal plan.
- AR :: "Timebox Your Day".
Agreement and divergence: the against side is real: Allen keeps the
calendar for the hard landscape only and forbids daily to-do lists on it;
Forster says "don't use this system for things at specific times";
Carroll and Cirillo keep the list as the plan; Burkeman fixes the hours
but not the boxes.  Inside the for side, precision ranges from every
minute (Newport 2013) through three box sizes (Zao-Sanders) to a single
protected block (Knapp, Keller).  Newport's own corpus carries the
tension: "every minute a job" against "simplify your workday".
Best formulation: Newport's revision columns, because they make the
re-plan the practice rather than the failure; Zao-Sanders' three sizes
for the parts that are not deep.
Evidence: Zao-Sanders' internal ranking of 100 hacks (self-report);
Newport's "40 blocked hours equal 60 unstructured" (unverified claim).
Decided by the person: which discretionary blocks exist today and what
goes in the first one.
Derivable: fixed events, autopilot blocks and theme days from the
template, re-flow after a slip, durations from history, whether the block
survived (calendar diff).  The inventory's schedule.org is the template
in prose; org-timegrid writes blocks; the queue packs against capacity.

**** Fixed capacity and slack  (convergence: 14)
A day and a week hold a known number of usable hours, measured rather
than asserted, and a plan that fills them all is wrong by lunchtime.
- POM :: "Available Pomodoros: 8", set from yesterday's count.
- TA :: 168 hours as the budget; "there are no typical weeks".
- SP :: work 20% under capacity; double the honest estimate.
- ESS :: add 50% to every estimate; "protect the asset".
- TBP :: "I leave some extra room next to my time blocks".
- DW :: about four deep hours a day is the ceiling.
- MSP :: if the template cannot hold the deep work, fix the role.
- MF :: DIT sizes the day before it starts; three unfinished lists trigger the three-cause audit.
- 4KW :: fixed volume; predetermined time boundaries.
- 12WY :: buffer blocks, 30-60 minutes once or twice a day.
- TBX :: what gets no box does not happen.
- EM :: 90-120 minute cycles; a real break between.
- PK :: "the closer you get to capacity, the more stress taxes the brain".
- R3 :: "you'll dramatically over and underestimate how much capacity you have".
Agreement and divergence: all agree capacity is finite and most agree it
is smaller than it feels.  Cirillo and Vanderkam derive it from the
record; Newport and McKeown pad estimates instead; Forster reads it off
failure.  The user's own schedule.org already states the rule: "focus is a
budget, not a well", four hours per block at most.
Best formulation: Cirillo's "available pomodoros", because capacity taken
from yesterday's actual is the only version that self-corrects.
Evidence: Newport's four-hour ceiling is practitioner report; the
ultradian figure is sleep research applied loosely to waking work.
Decided by the person: the stop time and the slack fraction.
Derivable: capacity per weekday from the history of =PROG= intervals,
the overcommitment report, the estimate correction.  The inventory's
org-queue-capacity is a labelled guess (five hours) waiting on a
fortnight of transitions.

**** Work-in-progress limit: closed list, pull, holding tank  (convergence: 13)
A hard count on what is active at once; new work enters only when
something leaves; the rest is visibly parked.
- PK :: "limit WIP", 2-3 for one person; pull, never push; the pen has its own cap.
- SP :: at most three active projects; an unbounded holding tank; queue position told to the requester.
- 4KW :: three in progress; a closed list of ten fed from the open list.
- MF :: the closed will-do list; one current initiative at a time.
- ONE :: one thing at every horizon.
- IVY :: six, and serially.
- 12WY :: one to three goals per twelve weeks.
- OKR :: three to five key results.
- FTVS :: one bet per cycle; the circuit breaker refuses extension.
- ESS :: the 90-out-of-100 gate at intake.
- AR :: three wins at every scale.
- WWE :: the "This week" column.
- DW :: the bimodal philosophy: whole days for one thing.
Agreement and divergence: the numbers agree (one, three, ten) and the
reasons do not.  The useful difference is where the limit binds: at the
moment of starting (Benson, Burkeman), at planning (Forster, Moran, Doerr,
Singer), or at commitment (McKeown, Keller).  Benson's is the only one
that is always on.
Best formulation: Personal Kanban, because a limit enforced at the moment

Evidence: none beyond practitioner report (Benson's stress claim is

Decided by the person: what to demote when the limit binds.
Derivable: the count, the "pull the next one" prompt, days spent in

**** Asynchronous execution: the agent chain  (convergence: 0, and that is the finding)
Work is done by an agent you kick off and review; several run at once;
each returns in minutes with a result that needs your attention; the
person is the constraint, not the executor.
- GTD :: Waiting For is the nearest primitive: delegated, dated, chased; built for days, and for answers rather than work product.
- WWE :: "Waiting to hear back" as a column; the process principle (design the workflow, do not react to each message) is the right instinct at the wrong scale.
- PK :: the pen, capped so it does not become a hidden WIP column; pull when a slot frees.
- SP :: push work to others with an intake reply; the holding tank; "do fewer things".
- DW :: attention is deployed sequentially; batch the shallow; the sender filter.
- POM :: an arrival is an external interruption: inform, negotiate, call back at the ring.
- IVY :: the night-before list, rewritten as tomorrow's kickoffs.
- FTVS :: the appetite and the circuit breaker, per chain.
- EM :: analytic work (review) at the peak, generative work (prompting) at the rebound.
Agreement and divergence: no system in the survey describes it, because
none was written for it.  What they supply are the parts: a delegation
list, a WIP limit enforced at the moment of starting, a rule that
interruptions are logged and served later, a next-action-only discipline
that keeps speculative work small, and a night-before ritual.  What none
supplies is the arithmetic: with agent latency L and your own
spec-plus-review time H per cycle, you are saturated once chains exceed
about 1 + L/H.  At ten to twenty minute runs and twenty minutes of your
own that is two, rising to three as runs stretch toward half an hour;
the composite's three, reached by a fourth route, is the ceiling and two
is the working number.
Best formulation: none to cite.  Benson's WIP limit at the moment of
starting, Cirillo's interruption rule and Allen's next-action discipline
composed, with Goldratt's theory of constraints (outside the survey) as
the frame: keep the constraint busy on the highest-value work, buffer in
front of it, subordinate everything else to it.
Evidence: the arithmetic, and Little's law, which Benson cites.
Decided by the person: the prompt (which is writing a task), the review
verdict (accept, iterate, abandon), and the chain limit.
Derivable: kick and land times, latency, review lag, iterations per
chain, human against machine minutes, the expected landings of the day,
and the moment to review.  The inventory has a Stop hook already routed
to zetta-notify and nothing that reads it.

happens to them over time.

- ZTD :: optional; then Simplify: halve the list, and halve it again.
- PK :: Options, not To Do, because "To Do implies obligation".
- WWE :: Back burner; no purge rule.
- SP :: the holding tank, unbounded but visible; renegotiable.
- 4KW :: the open list, kept but never worked from.
- MT :: the might-do list, never groomed; things are simply not chosen.
- MF :: "not keen on Someday/Maybes"; dismissal is the answer.
- FTVS :: no backlog: "really important ideas will come back to you".
- ESS :: keeping options is the endowment effect; uncommit.
- ONE :: everything that is not the ONE Thing is not tracked.
- BUJO :: the Future Log; migration decides what survives.
- EIS :: Q2 as a holding pen; Q4 deleted.
- EN :: "if a note doesn't seem interesting after a few looks, archive or delete it".
- IR :: postpone, suspend, tune down.
Agreement and divergence: this is the most contested concept in the
survey.  Four positions: keep and review (Allen), keep and ignore
(Burkeman, Knapp), keep with decay (Forster, Matuschak, Wozniak), and do

the promise, not to the record: Burkeman calls the promise anxiety
management, Forster calls it "unfulfilled wishes".
Best formulation: Benson's semantics (options carry no obligation) with
Forster's dismissal (pruning as a side effect of processing) and

Evidence: none beyond practitioner report.
Decided by the person: the re-decision when an item resurfaces.
Derivable: age, times surfaced, never-pulled, duplicate capture, the

**** Deadlines and urgency  (convergence: 10)
A deadline is a date by which something must be finished; urgency is a
property computed from it and the estimate, not a feeling to cultivate or
a label to assign.
- GTD :: the calendar is the hard landscape; a date and nothing more.
- MF :: "urgency relates to when the task needs to be started", not finished.
- FTF :: shrink urgency by living in Quadrant II.
- EIS :: urgent means "do first", eight per cell.
- FROG :: cultivate "a sense of urgency" as a virtue.
- TBX/TA :: Parkinson: set the box tighter than the estimate.
- FTVS :: the appetite and the circuit breaker; no extension by default.
- 12WY :: the compressed year as an urgency device.
- SP :: double the timeline; "take longer"; a natural pace.
- ESS :: buffers of 50%.
Agreement and divergence: three camps.  Urgency as virtue (Tracy, Moran),
urgency as pathology (Covey, Newport), urgency as arithmetic (Allen,
Forster).  The Parkinson camp uses artificial tightness deliberately, the
Newport camp deliberately loosens.  Forster's definition is the one that
turns a deadline into a schedule: start-by equals deadline minus duration.
Best formulation: Forster's start-time definition on Allen's hard
landscape, with a hard/soft distinction so that "would be nice by Friday"
cannot outrank work that must ship.
Evidence: none beyond practitioner report.
Decided by the person: whether a date is hard or soft, at the moment it
is written.
Derivable: start-by, the urgency curve, overdue, the spread of a long
task across the days before its deadline.  The inventory has
DEADLINE_TYPE, a convex urgency term, and deadline backpressure as the one
unbuilt milestone.

**** Priority: decided up front, emergent, or leverage  (convergence: 13)
How the order of work is determined: by labels assigned in advance, by a
computation over facts, by a leverage test at intake, or by intuition at
the moment.
- FROG :: ABCDE re-labelled daily; never a B before an A.
- IVY :: rank six every evening.
- EIS :: sort each task on arrival into a cell.
- FTF :: importance judged weekly against mission and role.
- ONE :: the focusing question, "easier or unnecessary", as the test.
- ESS :: score 0-100 on the single criterion; under 90 becomes 0.
- IR :: a 0-100% priority per element, and auto-postpone below it.
- 12WY :: tactics ranked by the plan.
- GTD :: priority last, by gut; "you can't decide priority in a vacuum".
- MF :: "don't prioritise your to-do list"; standing out; the FVP "want" chain.
- PK :: "thoughtful prioritisation and completion beats rigorous up-front planning"; an optional colour.
- AR :: outcomes as the lens; MUST/SHOULD/COULD on Monday.
- 4KW :: avoid the middling twenty "at all costs".
Agreement and divergence: the up-front camp (Tracy, Lee, the matrix,
Moran) re-labels constantly and the labels decay until everything is an

Forster's own: importance decides what enters the list, and once
committed, everything must be done and urgency orders it.
Best formulation: importance at intake (McKeown's single criterion,

Evidence: none beyond practitioner report.
Decided by the person: an IMPACT value once, if at all; the NEXT

Derivable: the whole running order from deadline, impact, age, state,
effort and category.  The inventory's queue computes exactly this;
absent priority is a real value; the cookie is faded to a hint; IMPACT

**** Migration, pruning, dismissal, and deciding what to fail at  (convergence: 17)

system, and the cadence at which the question is asked.
- BUJO :: monthly and yearly migration; "if it isn't worth the effort to rewrite ... get rid of it".
- MF :: dismissal: a page passed cold is dismissed; weeding; the DIT audit cuts commitments.
- GTD :: delete from Someday/Maybe at the weekly review.
- POM :: activities that "lose their purpose" are deleted; the day sheet is rebuilt.
- TBX :: no box, no existence.
- 4KW :: "decide in advance what to fail at"; strategic underachievement.
- ESS :: zero-based budgeting, the reverse pilot, naming sunk cost.
- FTVS :: no backlog to prune.
- ZTD :: Simplify, a scheduled habit.
- REV :: Ferriss's not-to-do list; YearCompass's "letting go".
- EN :: archive or delete after a few looks.
- AR :: the fresh start: the undone is not carried unless re-chosen.
- IVY :: carry-over must re-compete for a slot.
- IR :: auto-postpone, suspend, date-stamped expiry.
- PARA :: archive, never delete.
- FTF :: say no to Quadrant III "pleasantly, smilingly, nonapologetically".

Agreement and divergence: every system that keeps a list prunes it, and

(Singer).  Carroll is explicit that the friction exists to force the

Decided by the person: the answer to "still worth it?".
Derivable: the question, its timing, and the evidence attached to it.

REVIEW_ON is a tickler.

**** Estimation and calibration  (convergence: 8)

records sheet, or here a state log), and the difference corrects future

- POM :: estimate in pomodoros; the Records Sheet; a second estimate is a signal, a third means reconsider.

- FTVS :: an appetite starts with a number; an estimate ends with one.

shown back as a strength rather than a chain, and kept out of the task
list.
- AH :: cue, craving, response, reward; four laws; the stack; never miss twice; the tracker; identity.
- TH :: B = MAP; "After I [ANCHOR], I will [TINY BEHAVIOR]", then celebrate; no tracking.
- POH :: cue, routine, reward; five cue categories; keystone habits.
- HT :: chains, strength scores, reserves, fresh starts; the display drives persistence.
- IND :: pacts (effort, price, identity); the ten-minute rule.
- EM :: positive energy rituals; the break every 90-120 minutes.
- ZTD :: one habit at a time, thirty days each; morning, evening and weekly routines.
- ONE :: the 66-day challenge.
- DW :: the deep-work ritual; the rhythmic philosophy's chain method.
- MSP/SP :: autopilot slots pinned to a day and a place.
- BUJO :: the habit tracker as a collection.
- TBP :: the daily metrics line.
- REV :: the Theme System's half-fillable circles.
- R3 :: the maintenance day as a batched routine.
Agreement and divergence: the design side converges on one model (cue

tracking side splits: Clear makes the tick a reward, Fogg refuses to
track, Habitica punishes the miss, the Bullet Journal draws a grid.  Fogg
says emotion wires the habit and Lally counts repetitions; Duhigg keeps
the cue and Wood changes the context.  Every member agrees a habit is not
a task: no deadline, no overdue, a time series not a done bit.

about 43% of daily action repeats in the same context.  Loop: a 13-day

Derivable: the strength score, the two-gap alert, anchor detection from

habits; the routine exists only as prose.

Decided by the person: the prompts (edited, not written from scratch) and

Derivable: neighbours surfaced while writing, on-this-day, decay-based
resurfacing of one's own notes, relevance to the current missions.  The
inventory has anki.el, a hybrid search service, a HyWiki graph and a day
search; nothing schedules a return.

**** The journal and log as a by-product  (convergence: 13)
The record of the day is produced by working, not by writing it up:
transitions, clock, state changes, the calendar corrected after the fact,
and a done list that fills itself.
- IJ :: one timestamped line per transition; journal, task log and time log in one artefact.
- DN :: the dated page as the spine; "the journal is the index".
- TBX :: the calendar is the record, if boxes are corrected.
- POM :: the Records Sheet: estimate, real, diff.
- TA :: the 168-hour log, back-filled three or four times a day.
- 4KW/FTVS :: the done list that starts empty each morning; Andreessen's anti-todo.
- PK :: the Done column with transition timestamps.
- MT :: the nightly Reflect note as a trial log.
- BUJO :: the daily log; the event bullet "logged after they occur".
- TBP :: the metrics line.
- REV :: Chua's review is generated from her own logs; Ferriss walks the calendar.
- AR :: daily outcomes are the input to Friday.
- DW :: the tally.
Agreement and divergence: the done list is used either as disposable
morale (Andreessen tears it up) or as retained diagnostic material
(Benson's retro, Meier's Friday, Cirillo's sheet); nobody keeps it for its
own sake.  Stubblebine's write is the only one that is also the thinking
(what next, what is in the way); everything else can come from
instruments.
Best formulation: Stubblebine's transition line as the one optional human
write, on Chua's principle that the review is generated from what the
system already records.
Evidence: Amabile's progress principle is cited for done lists; otherwise
practitioner report.
Decided by the person: the optional one-liner at a switch.
Derivable: timestamps, the previous task from the last transition into

time table with deltas.  The inventory logs every state change into
drawers (no clock is kept: the transitions are the clock), stamps
every capture, and the hub commits the whole kb daily; it has no daily
page and no transition template.

**** Identity, theme, purpose  (convergence: 14)
The top of the stack is a direction rather than a number: who you are
becoming, what the season is for, what you will say no to.
- AH :: identity-based habits; "every action is a vote"; the Integrity Report.
- TH :: the aspiration, separated from the behaviour.
- FTF :: the mission statement; compass before clock.
- GTD :: Horizon 5, purpose and principles.
- REV :: the Theme System, "a good theme can't fail"; YearCompass's word for the year.
- ONE :: the someday goal at the top of the ladder.
- ESS :: the essential intent, "one decision that makes a thousand".
- 12WY :: the vision as the reason to execute.
- SP :: lifestyle-centric planning; "productivity depends on scale".
- IND :: values as "attributes of the person you want to be"; three domains.
- BUJO :: "define why you want to bullet journal, not how".
- 4KW :: finitude; the joy of missing out.
- DM :: the screen: does the tool serve something you deeply value.
- AR :: hot spots as the portfolio.
Agreement and divergence: goal (numeric, can fail) against theme
(direction, cannot) against identity (votes) against values (a filter).
The annual reviewers split again: Ferriss derives next year from last
year's calendar, YearCompass dreams first.
Best formulation: the Theme System at the top, because a direction that
cannot fail costs one sentence a season and still filters; Clear's
identity framing for habits; McKeown's intent as the intake criterion.
Evidence: none beyond practitioner report.
Decided by the person: entirely; one sentence a season.
Derivable: alignment prompts seeded from the theme text, "did today serve
the theme" from the log.

**** The intake gate and saying no  (convergence: 12)
New commitments enter only against visible evidence of capacity, and the
refusal is a designed artefact rather than an act of will.
- ESS :: under 90 out of 100 is 0; "which problem do I want?"; the reverse pilot.
- SP :: no yes without calendar time; the intake reply with queue position and date; the overhead tax.
- FTVS :: the appetite; "Interesting. Maybe some day."
- FTF :: no to Quadrant III.
- ZTD :: say no; cut the information stream.
- DW :: become hard to reach; the sender filter.
- WWE :: protocols; make the sender do the work.
- 4KW :: burn bridges; decide what to fail at.
- IND :: schedule syncing with manager or partner.
- MF :: commitments versus interests; the DIT audit cuts commitments.
- PK :: the board shows "what you can promise, what you can say no to".
- ONE :: no to everything that is not the ONE Thing.
Agreement and divergence: all agree the no must be structural.  McKeown
gates by a score, Newport by calendar evidence and a queue, Singer by a
fixed appetite, Benson by a visible board.  The solo practitioner's
version of Newport's reply is a reply to oneself.
Best formulation: Newport's Slow Productivity intake, because it produces
the evidence (hours on the calendar, position in the queue) that makes the
no factual rather than brave; McKeown's threshold for the borderline.
Evidence: none beyond practitioner report.
Decided by the person: the no.
Derivable: queue position and ETA, the capacity evidence, the overhead
tax per project.  The inventory's overcommitment report is the evidence;
nothing yet asks the intake question.

What the ranking shows.  The top four are the skeleton every list-keeping
system shares: write it down, throw it away later, pick a few each day,
and look at the whole thing once a week.  A system with only those four
(Zen to Done is nearly that) is recognisably the same animal as GTD.  The
next tier, five through ten, is where the modern systems earn their
differences: allocating time rather than listing it, sizing the day to a
measured capacity, and treating habits and identity as first-class,
which the 2001-era action systems left to the reader.  The knowledge
concepts (23, 21, parts of 14) converge among a different subset, the
notes lineage and the SRS family, and the count understates them because
the action systems simply have no opinion about notes; Newport keeps no
note database at all.  Several counts are lineage rather than
independent arrival: big rocks were copied Covey -> Babauta -> Newport;
the rule of three is Meier -> Bailey; the two-minute rule is Allen ->
Babauta; Areas of Focus is Allen -> Forte; the weekly review is Allen ->
Forte, Carroll (monthly) and Newport; the chain is Seinfeld (by report) ->
Clear.  Discounting lineage, the independent arrivals are the striking
ones: the WIP limit (Toyota via Benson, Forster's closed list, Burkeman's
three, Newport's pull system, all separately), the closing ritual (Lee in
1918 by tradition, Carroll, Newport, Cirillo), pruning by processing
(Forster) against pruning by rewriting (Carroll), and the scheduler,
which nobody outside the SRS family has.  Where three unrelated lineages
land on the same number, three items in progress, the number deserves
more trust than any one of their arguments for it.

One concept has a convergence of zero, and it is the one that now
governs most of the working day: asynchronous execution by agents.  No
system in the survey was written for it.  Its entry in 2.1 is assembled
from parts the others supply, and Part 3's G16 is the first mechanism in
the document with no ancestor in Part 1.

** 2.3 The tensions

*** Exhaustive lists versus closed and short lists
Side A: GTD, BASB, Autofocus, Burkeman's open list, Benson's options.  The
head is an unreliable store; a complete inventory is what lets it let go;
Forster's catch-all "is about catching stuff coming at you".
Side B: Do It Tomorrow, Burkeman's closed list, Slow Productivity's active
three, Basecamp's no-backlog, Keller's success list, McKeown.  A long list
is a promise you cannot keep, and the promise is what harms; "for every
item you cross off you think of another three".
Resolution for this user: exhaustive capture, closed execution.  The
inbox stays universal because the requirement is that capture cost
nothing; the day is a closed list of what the queue proposed and the
person accepted, never the backlog itself.  The inventory already does
this: every working view discards IDEA and HOLD, the queue derives the
day from the open backlog each morning and reports what it cut.  From
side A, keep the record; from side B, never work from it directly, and
let dismissal by age quietly retire what never gets pulled.

*** Plan the time versus pick intuitively versus a computed queue
Side A: Newport, Zao-Sanders, Eyal, Keller.  A list omits how long things
take and where the free time is; only a plan on the calendar confronts
that; "to-do lists are a terrible daily planning tool".
Side B: Forster, Allen.  Rational prioritising subverts itself; the
moment knows what stands out; "the best order to do things in is the
order they present themselves"; context and energy first.
Resolution for this user: the routine supplies the shape, the queue
supplies the proposal, the person disposes.  schedule.org already fixes
two focus blocks, the dip and the admin slot; that is Newport's template
and it should stay rules, not a grid.  Inside a block, Forster is right:
pick what stands out from the short set the queue packed, and mark the
override as NEXT.  The queue is neither side's enemy: it is Allen's four
criteria and Forster's urgency-as-start-time computed instead of felt,
and its "why is this not on my day" report is what earns trust.  Never
give every minute a job; replan by re-running the packer, which is
Newport's revision column without the redrawing.

*** Friction as feature versus automation
Side A: Carroll.  Rewriting by hand forces a pause on every open item;
"if an entry isn't worth the effort to rewrite ... get rid of it".
Side B: daily notes, timeboxing's three sizes, Forte's thirty-minute
review, the requirement itself.  Friction is where systems die; the
weekly review is GTD's most-skipped step.
Resolution for this user: drop the transcription, keep the re-decision.
Carroll's friction is a mechanism for making you look at each item with
its history; the inventory can put that history in front of you for free
(the Neglected view's age from the newest timestamp anywhere in the
entry, the queue's carry-over count, times surfaced).  The re-decision
becomes a prompt with evidence attached, asked at the weekly review and
whenever a parked item's REVIEW_ON arrives, and answered in one
keystroke.  Nothing is ever rewritten by hand.

*** Top-down containers versus bottom-up links; tags versus no tags
Side A: PARA, Johnny.Decimal, Voit.  Fixed containers by actionability or
by number answer "where do I put this" without thought; a small
controlled vocabulary of tags, in the file name, survives every tool;
Voit's is the only position with controlled experiments behind it.
Side B: Zettelkasten, evergreen notes, LYT.  Structure must be earned;
folders encapsulate and limit; "tags are an ineffective association
structure"; add a structure note or MOC at the squeeze point, not before.
Resolution for this user: containers for tasks and artefacts, a flat
linked graph for ideas, and tags only from closed vocabularies.  Tasks
already live in per-area files with a category, which is PARA's
actionability partition without the folder moves (TODO states and archive
do the lifecycle).  Ideas live as HyWiki pages and org links, where
Matuschak's "let structure emerge" and Luhmann's link context apply, with
the hybrid search service and the graph view standing in for the
register.  Voit wins on the small closed sets (contexts, energy, people)
because classification against a dozen known labels is exactly what an
LLM decorates well; Matuschak wins on topics, which search and links
answer better than any tag a person would type.  Johnny.Decimal's
numbering is refused: the address is the file path and the WikiWord.

*** Streaks versus forgiveness
Side A: the chain, Streaks, Habitica, Clear's tracker as a reward.  An
intact run motivates; "don't break the chain".
Side B: Clear's never miss twice, Lally ("missing one opportunity did not
materially affect"), Loop's smoothing, Sharif & Shu's reserves, Beshears'
flexibility, Fogg's refusal to track.
Resolution for this user: a strength score with a repair window, an alert
on two consecutive misses, and no display that resets to zero.  Silverman
& Barasch show that both sides are describing the display, not the
behaviour: an intact streak helps and a broken one hurts regardless of
what you did, so the design choice is which display to show.  Sharif &
Shu's seven-days-with-two-skips is the goal shape; Loop's thirteen-day
half-life is the number.

*** Deadlines as facts versus deadlines as motivation
Side A: Allen ("a date and nothing more"), Newport (a deadline is a fact
about the day), Forster (start-time arithmetic).
Side B: Tracy (cultivate urgency), Moran (the compressed year), Parkinson
used deliberately by Zao-Sanders and Vanderkam.
Resolution for this user: a deadline binds only when it is real, a soft
date pulls at a third of the weight, and no date is invented to motivate.
The queue already treats a hard deadline as a commitment and a soft one
as a pull; what it lacks is Forster's start-time, which is the deadline
backpressure milestone.  Parkinson is used through the box and the
appetite (a block has an end; a project has an appetite), never through
a fake due date, because a fake date would poison the one signal the
scorer trusts.

*** Priority up front versus emergent
Side A: Tracy's ABCDE daily, Lee's ranking, the matrix on arrival, Moran's
plan.  Decide once, then obey; never a B before an A.
Side B: Allen (priority last, by gut), Forster (never), Benson
(completion beats planning), Meier (outcomes as the lens).
Resolution for this user: importance once at intake, everything else
computed, one human override.  The IMPACT property is Covey's importance
axis separated from urgency and set by a single keypress if at all;
absent is neutral.  The running order comes from the scorer (deadline,
impact, age, state, effort, category glut), which is Allen's four
criteria without the vacuum.  NEXT is the override, and it is a
commitment rather than a bonus precisely so it cannot be out-argued by a
near deadline.  ABCDE relabelling is refused because labels decay until
everything is an A; the priority cookie stays as a faded hint.

*** Habits inside the task system versus a separate store
Side A: repeating tasks, BuJo's tracker collection, Newport's metrics line
on the plan page.  One system, one page.
Side B: the habits cluster's cross-notes: a habit has no deadline, no
overdue state, no rollover, a strength rather than a done bit, and must
not spawn a fresh instance on completion.
Resolution for this user: a separate store and a chart, and the routine
supplies the anchors.  Habits give the day its shape (the lift, the bike,
the reading hour) and cost fixed time that the capacity calculation must
subtract, but they never enter the queue as candidates and never appear
in a backlog view.  Their record is a time series read at the weekly
review.  Fogg's anchor, not Clear's clock time, is the key, following
Beshears.

*** Estimate everything versus estimate nothing
Side A: Cirillo, Zao-Sanders, Vanderkam, Newport's "learn your real
durations".  Without an estimate a day cannot be sized, and estimates
only improve when checked.
Side B: Carroll, Stubblebine, Knapp.  Estimating is bookkeeping that
does not survive contact; a Highlight is 60-90 minutes and that is all
the sizing needed.
Resolution for this user: estimate optionally, coarsely, at the only
moment it is cheap, and let the actuals correct it.  The t capture
template's one-keypress effort list is Zao-Sanders' three sizes with a
few more rungs; a task with no estimate is still planned against a
default and marked as guessed; the calibration factor per category is
Cirillo's Records Sheet computed rather than transcribed.  Never block a
task on an estimate, and treat a task re-estimated twice as Cirillo does,
as a signal to reconsider it.

*** Stop when the unit ends versus keep going
Side A: Cirillo.  "If a Pomodoro Begins, It Has to Ring"; the unit is
indivisible so that it can be counted and estimated against.
Side B: Deep Work, the Highlight, Keller's four hours, Forster's "as long
as you feel like".  Flow is the point; an arbitrary bell is an
interruption you scheduled yourself.
Resolution for this user: pomodoros where focus is weakest, open blocks
where it is strongest.  schedule.org already decides this: structured
units in the first two hours of block one and through the post-lunch dip,
"vague" work after.  The unit is kept for the dip, where "structure
carries what willpower won't"; measurement comes from the state
transitions, not from the bell; the deep block is never rung.  Pink's break rules apply between blocks.

*** Goal-laden year versus goal-free theme
Side A: YearCompass, Clear's categories with numbers, OKRs, the 12-week
year.  A number can be checked; execution can be scored.
Side B: Grey & Hurley's theme ("a good theme can't fail"), Newport's
lifestyle picture, Burkeman's strategic underachievement, Ferriss's
evidence-first review.
Resolution for this user: a theme at the top, at most three missions a
season, and an annual review that walks the record rather than the
imagination.  OKR self-grading is refused on Gothelf's argument that a
grader who is also the setter produces gameable numbers; execution
percentages are refused because they measure activity.  YearCompass's
"letting go" and Ferriss's two-column calendar walk are kept because the
inventory has the archive to walk (the state log with its intervals,
the daily kb commit); the dream-first half is dropped.

*** Keep a backlog versus "important ideas come back"
Side A: GTD, Benson, Newport's tank, Forster's own "never be without a
means of recording ideas".
Side B: Basecamp: backlogs waste the time spent grooming them; Andreessen
tears up the card; Keller and McKeown keep nothing.
Resolution for this user: keep it as options with decay, and use
Basecamp's test as a signal rather than a policy.  IDEA and HOLD exist,
are hidden from every working view, and carry a REVIEW_ON tickler; an
item that has surfaced N times without being pulled is dismissed in
Forster's sense (marked, not deleted).  An idea captured twice is the
Basecamp event: it came back, and duplicate detection should say so and
raise it.  Nothing is ever groomed by hand.

*** The done list kept versus torn up
Side A: Benson's Done column, Meier's Friday, Cirillo's sheet, the 12-week
scorecard.  The record is the raw material for the retrospective and for
calibration.
Side B: Andreessen, Burkeman's list that empties overnight, Bailey.
Marvel, then destroy; the morale is the point.
Resolution for this user: kept, because keeping it costs nothing.  The
state log already is the done list; the shutdown ritual
shows it for Andreessen's reason and the weekly review reads it for
Benson's.  Nothing is written for it.

*** Sequential attention versus pipelined execution
Side A: Deep Work, time-block planning, the pomodoro.  Attention is
deployed sequentially; a switch leaves residue; the day is a few
unbroken blocks and nothing is checked inside them.
Side B: the agent workflow as it is lived.  Several chains in flight;
each returns in ten to twenty minutes; a result rots if it waits
(direction changes, context fades, the next prompt cannot be written);
an idle agent is wasted capacity.
Resolution for this user: the person stays sequential and the agents
are pipelined, and the two meet only at unit boundaries.  Kick off at
block starts, before the lift, and at the close (overnight, headless);
review at pomodoro breaks and block ends, never mid-unit; let a landing
wait, since with three chains running an idle agent costs little and a
broken deep unit costs a lot.  Keep one prompt ahead per chain and no
more, so a review that changes direction flushes at most one prompt.
Cap chains in flight at three, which is where the arithmetic says you
become the queue.  Write prompts in the evening (Pink's rebound, Ivy
Lee's night before) and review in the morning peak.  The only landing
allowed to interrupt is an agent's question, and only quietly, because
until it is answered that chain is idle.  From side A, the blocks and
the residue argument; from side B, the pipeline and its buffers.

** 2.4 The composite system

*** Primitives
- Item :: anything captured: raw text, a timestamp, a source link.  It
  acquires facets afterwards, almost all of them derived: kind (task,
  note, question, event, reference), state, area, project, context,
  energy, effort, dates, impact.  Provenance: GTD's In, BuJo's rapid log,
  the daily page, Voit's "generate the metadata".
- Commitment or option :: the one distinction that matters about an item.
  A commitment is in a working state (TODO, NEXT, PROG, WAIT, QUES) and
  can be planned; an option (IDEA, HOLD) is parked with a review date,
  hidden from every working view, and decays.  NEXT is a commitment made
  by a person rather than a date.  Provenance: Benson's options,
  Newport's tank, Burkeman's open and closed lists, Forster's dismissal.
- Project :: an outcome that needs more than one session.  It is live only
  if it has a next step or a block; otherwise it is dormant and says so.
  Deep projects receive time directly; shallow ones decompose.
  Provenance: GTD's project and invariant, Newport's full-horizon rule,
  Forte's "a project without a goal is a hobby".
- Area :: an ongoing standard with no end, a minimum and a maximum; the
  container an item belongs to and the axis balance is judged on.
  Resources are what you are interested in, not responsible for.
  Provenance: PARA, Meier's hot spots, Covey's roles, Allen's Horizon 2.
- Horizon :: a theme for the season or year that cannot fail, at most
  three missions for the season, the week's one to three rocks, today's
  one to three picks; each read only at the scale below.  Provenance:
  Newport's multi-scale planning, the Theme System, Burkeman's three.
- Block :: a span of the day from the routine template, fixed (the lift,
  the admin slot) or discretionary (the focus blocks).  A pomodoro is the
  unit inside a block where focus is weak; a deep block is never rung.
  Provenance: Newport's template and revision columns, Zao-Sanders'
  sizes, Cirillo, Pink, schedule.org.
- Habit :: an anchor, a tiny behaviour, a celebration, and a strength
  score with a repair window.  Not an item: no deadline, no overdue, no
  rollover; it costs fixed time in the capacity and never enters the
  queue.  Provenance: Fogg, Clear, Loop, Lally, Sharif & Shu, Beshears.
- Note :: fleeting (an item), literature (a highlight or annotation),
  permanent (a page in your own words with a stated reason on each link);
  structure added at the squeeze point; returned by a scheduler when it
  must be remembered and by linking when it must be thought.  Provenance:
  Ahrens, Luhmann, Matuschak, Milo, Wozniak, Forte's favourite problems.
- Chain :: an outcome pursued through agent runs.  Each run is kicked
  (a prompt, written by you), in flight (machine time, not yours),
  landed (a result that is now a commitment to review) and reviewed
  (accept, iterate with the next prompt, or abandon).  A chain carries
  exactly one ready prompt ahead and a coarse outline of the rest; at
  most three chains are in flight; a chain that has iterated past its
  appetite is stopped and reshaped.  Provenance: none in the survey;
  assembled from Allen's next action, Benson's WIP limit and pull,
  Cirillo's interruption rule, Ivy Lee's night before, Shape Up's
  circuit breaker, Goldratt's constraint.

*** Rituals
- Capture :: any moment; two keystrokes; raw text lands in the one inbox
  with a timestamp and a backlink.  The person decides only that it is
  worth a jot.  Provenance: GTD, BuJo, Newport's collection columns,
  Cirillo's glyphs.
- Clarify :: when the inbox is opened, daily or so, in one sitting.  The
  decorated item is shown with its proposed kind, area, context, effort
  and dates; the person confirms or corrects with a keystroke and decides
  commitment or option and where it lives.  Provenance: GTD's flowchart,
  Benson's pull, Forte's "organise later".
- Open the day :: in the routine's planning slot, five minutes.  The
  template has drawn the fixed blocks; the queue proposes a packed day
  and says what it cut; the person accepts, moves, or overrides with
  NEXT, and chooses the one to three picks that will make the day;
  the prompts written the night before are kicked off first, so the
  agents are running before the plan is read.  Provenance: Newport's
  daily plan, Cirillo's available pomodoros,
  Babauta's MITs, Make Time's Highlight, Ivy Lee.
- Work the block :: pull one thing and move it to =PROG=, which is the
  clock; jot interruptions without
  leaving; write one line at a switch if it helps; ring pomodoros in the
  dip and nowhere else; at each unit boundary, land what has landed:
  review, then accept, iterate with the next prompt, or abandon.  The
  person decides what stands out from the packed set, and the verdict on
  each landing.  Provenance: Forster, Cirillo, Stubblebine, Newport,
  Pink.
- Close the day :: at the fixed stop.  A generated checklist (unread,
  unfiled, overdue, tomorrow's fixed events), the done list, carry-over
  applied, tomorrow drafted, and every active chain shown with or
  without its next prompt.  The person picks tomorrow's one to three,
  writes the missing prompts (the overnight ones marked to run headless
  before dawn), and says the phrase.  Provenance: Newport's shutdown, Lee, Carroll's PM
  reflection, Andreessen's card.
- Weekly review :: thirty minutes, on the fresh-start day.  A generated
  pack: what moved, what stalled and for how long, waiting-fors by age,
  inbox count, projects without a next step, areas below their minimum,
  estimate versus actual, the week's calendar against the template.  The
  person re-chooses next week's rocks, answers "still worth it?" for each
  item the pack raises, and drops.  Provenance: Allen, Chua, Meier,
  Eyal, Benson's retro, Forte's brevity.
- Season :: once a quarter, an hour.  Set the theme, at most three
  missions, and the template's rules and quotas; walk the record
  (calendar, log, commits) into what to do more of and a not-to-do list.
  The yearly version is the same with a longer walk.  Provenance:
  Newport's strategic plan and template, the Theme System, Ferriss,
  YearCompass's letting go, Slow Productivity's seasons.

*** Rules
1. Capture is never the place to decide.  (GTD, BASB, daily notes)
2. Every live project has a next step or a block; otherwise it is dormant
   and labelled so.  (GTD, Newport's full-horizon rule)
3. Three: picks a day, projects in progress, missions a season.
   (Burkeman, Benson, Newport, Meier)
4. A hard deadline is a fact; a soft date pulls; no date is invented to
   motivate.  (Allen, Newport, Forster)
5. Importance is set once at intake, if at all; order is computed; NEXT
   is the only override.  (McKeown, Allen's four criteria, Forster)
6. Plan the shape, not the minutes; the plan is a hypothesis; replan
   without guilt.  (Newport's revision columns, Eyal)
7. Capacity is measured, not asserted, and a fifth of it is left empty.
   (Cirillo, Newport, Vanderkam)
8. Nothing is rewritten by hand; anything stale is shown with its age and
   asked "still worth it?".  (Carroll's re-decision without the friction,
   Forster's dismissal)
9. A missed habit is noise; two in a row is a signal; nothing resets to
   zero.  (Clear, Lally, Loop, Silverman & Barasch)
10. Deep work in the first block; pomodoros where focus is weakest;
    shallow work drains into the admin slot, never into focus time.
    (Newport, Pink, schedule.org)
11. Own words and link reasons are written by a person; classification
    against a small known list is not.  (Ahrens, Matuschak, Voit)
12. The day is closed; an unclosed day is an open loop.  (Newport, Lee)
13. One prompt ahead per chain; three chains in flight; a landing waits
    for the unit boundary.  (Allen's next action, Benson, Cirillo, the
    latency arithmetic)
14. Human minutes are planned; agent minutes are counted; the two never
    share a capacity.  (Goldratt, outside the survey)

*** What the composite refuses
- Hand-rewritten migration (BuJo) :: the pause is kept as a prompt with
  evidence; the copying is pure cost.
- An index and threading (BuJo, Johnny.Decimal's JDex) :: backlinks,
  search and the graph view compile it.
- Streak resets and gamified penalties (Streaks, Habitica) :: the display
  drives the behaviour, and a reset demotivates regardless of conduct.
- ABCDE or A/B/C relabelling every day (Tracy, Lee, the matrix) :: labels
  decay until everything is an A; the scorer re-ranks from facts.
- A separate habit app :: the anchors are in the routine and the record
  belongs with the rest of the log; only the store is separate.
- OKR self-grading and execution percentages (OKR, 12WY) :: a grader who
  is also the setter produces gameable numbers; percent measures activity.
- Johnny.Decimal numbering :: the path and the WikiWord are the address;
  renumbering is forbidden by the method and needed by life.
- Per-role Trello boards and a weekly narrative per role (WWE, MSP) ::
  the overhead tax Newport warns about, applied to his own apparatus;
  the agenda views are the boards.
- A paper planner and copying the calendar onto it (TBP) :: pure
  re-writing; the calendar is already text.
- "No capture at all" (Basecamp, Keller, McKeown) :: the requirement is
  cheap capture; the Basecamp insight survives as duplicate detection.
- Deep-work shame tallies (DW 2016) :: Newport dropped them himself;
  Slow Productivity asks for 20% under capacity instead.
- Every minute a job (TBP 2013) :: the routine fixes the shape; the rest
  is a hypothesis the queue can re-flow.
- Folgezettel and forced atomicity (ZK orthodoxy) :: Luhmann's own cards
  were not atomic; a claim-shaped title and a link reason are enough.
- The three-week caffeine-free hourly energy log (Bailey) :: the state
  log's intervals and the commit log already say when the good hours are.
- A hand-kept 168-hour audit and Records Sheet (Vanderkam, Cirillo) ::
  the state log and the git timeline are the audit.
- YearCompass's dream-first half and "magical triplets" :: the
  evidence-first half is kept; the rest is resolutions.
- Progressive summarization by bold and highlight (BASB) :: highlights
  arrive from readwise and pdfnote already; the only distillation worth
  a person's time is the own-words layer.

*** The primitives, as they stand

- Item :: A heading.  The =n= template writes =* %?= with a =:CREATED: %U=
  drawer; =N= adds =%a=; =t= adds a TODO keyword, =%^G= tags and an
  =%^{Effort}p= chosen from =Effort_ALL=.  Nothing else is derived.  The
  inbox is in =org-agenda-files= (=zetta-org-inbox-file=) and in the rolo
  corpus, so a raw item is already searchable, planable and greppable.
- Commitment or option :: The working states are =TODO NEXT PROG WAIT QUES=;
  the parked states are =HOLD= and =IDEA=, both excluded by
  =org-queue-excluded-states= and discarded by every backlog, context,
  project and horizon view.  =REVIEW_ON= (read by
  =zetta-org-agenda-review-due-p= and =org-queue-harvest--review-on=) is the
  tickler that brings a parked item back.  Leaving into =WAIT QUES HOLD=
  asks for a note, so the reason survives.  What is absent: an item never
  pulled has no age-based fate.
- Project :: A heading with TODO children.  The Projects view groups by
  =:auto-parent= and names "Standalone".  =:BLOCKED_BY:= holds an ID the
  core filters on.  The Capacity view files anything over three hours under
  "probably a project".  There is no test that a project has a startable
  child, and nothing marks a project dormant.
- Area :: The nine =(todo)= files, each meant to carry =#+CATEGORY=
  (=zetta-org-insert-todo-header=); category drives the Areas view and the
  glut penalty.  =~/kb/notes= is the resources layer (diet, finplan,
  curriculum, guitar).  No file states a minimum or maximum, so "an area
  below its floor" cannot be computed.
- Horizon :: Absent.  "the time in a day.org" names 1/2/5-year look-aheads
  and a curriculum, and =(todo) todo.org= holds the worry about bookkeeping;
  neither is read by anything.
- Block :: Appointments are plain active timestamps harvested with
  repeaters rolled forward; org-timegrid draws a week and writes a real
  timestamp on drag; calfw shows the month.  The routine (05:00 clean,
  05:30 plan, 06:00 lift, 08:00-12:00 block one, 12:00 bike, 13:30-17:00
  block two with pomodoros through the dip, 18:00-20:00 admin, 20:00 read,
  21:00 sleep) lives in prose in =~/kb/notes/schedule.org=;
  =org-queue-capacity= is five hours on a weekday and labelled a guess.
- Habit :: Absent.  =org-habit= ships with Org and is not loaded; the
  logging prerequisites (=org-log-done 'time=, =org-log-into-drawer t=) are
  already met, so the consistency graph would work the day it is enabled.
- Note :: Literature notes arrive from org-remark, readwise (kindle,
  Snipd, web, voicenotes) and pdfnote; permanent notes are HyWiki pages in
  =~/kb/wiki= (four exist) with WikiWords buttonized everywhere, a page
  seeded on first follow, and hywiki-graph for the neighbourhood; irs
  answers "what else is near this".  Nothing promotes an inbox note to a
  page, nothing asks why a link exists, nothing schedules a return.
- Chain :: Absent.  Agent sessions are started by hand in a terminal,
  their prompts live in the session, and their return is a desktop
  notification from the Stop hook that nothing in org reads.  How many
  are in flight, when each was kicked, how long it ran and how long the
  result waited for review are all unknown.

*** The rituals, as they stand

- Capture :: ",-o-o" then a letter; two keystrokes to a raw heading.
  Mobile arrives through Syncthing, voice through readwise voicenotes.
  Matches the composite exactly.
- Clarify :: ",-o-v i" shows the inbox split into "not yet a task" and "a
  task, refile it".  From there every facet is a separate gesture:
  =C-c C-t= state, =C-c C-x e= effort, =C-c C-q= tags, =C-c C-d=
  deadline, =C-c C-w= refile.  Column view (=C-c C-x C-c=) makes bulk
  triage single keys.  Nothing proposes anything.
- Open the day :: ",-o-Q" packs the day from the backlog, commitments
  first, and says what it cut; =N= on a heading marks the human pick.  The
  day's shape is in your head from schedule.org, not on screen.
- Work the block :: =C-c C-t p= (or ",-o-t") moves the entry to =PROG=,
  and that transition, logged with its timestamp, is the start of the
  work; leaving =PROG= is the end.  =org-clock= is configured but unused,
  so =CLOCKSUM= and the calibration read empty.  Capture is available
  from any buffer; the Context view (=c=) answers "what fits now".  A
  capture made while something is =PROG= does not know what it
  interrupted; an entry left in =PROG= overnight is not noticed.
- Kick off and land :: Nothing.  The day is already shaped by them; the
  system does not know.
- Close the day :: Nothing.  The queue's carry-over is applied the next
  morning, which is where the composite's shutdown would already have
  applied it.
- Weekly review :: The pieces exist as separate agenda commands (=r= what
  moved, =n= neglected, =W= chase, =u= untriaged, =I= parked, =E= capacity,
  =h= horizon, =P= priority) plus ",-o-C" calibration and the census.  No
  command runs them together, and nothing asks the one question the
  review is for.
- Season :: Nothing.

** 3.2 The gaps, ranked by leverage

Ranking rule: (bookkeeping removed per occurrence) x (occurrences per
week) / (build size).  A daily gesture saved beats a quarterly one; a
setting beats a package; a package with a pure core beats a view.  Where
two gaps tie, the one the others depend on goes first.

*** G1 AI decoration of the inbox  (rank 1; size M)
- Delivers :: Clarify; C1, C2, R1, R11.  The contract (fields, prompt,
  candidates, acceptance, tests) is specified by the sibling pass; this
  entry fixes its place in the config.
- Mechanism :: A zettapkg package =org-decorate= in org-queue's shape:
  =org-decorate-core.el= (pure: takes an entry plist, the candidate
  lists, the calibration table and a function that answers a prompt;
  returns a proposal plist), =org-decorate-harvest.el= (reads the entry,
  collects candidates: refile targets from =org-refile-targets=, the
  context and energy groups from =org-tag-alist=, categories from the
  agenda files, effort from =org-queue= calibration history, nearest
  headings from =irs_hybrid_search=), =org-decorate.el= (commands).  The
  model call goes through =gptel-request= with a preset; the model
  chooses among candidates, never free text.  Proposals are written as
  properties on the inbox entry: =AI_KIND=, =AI_KEYWORD=,
  =AI_TARGET=, =AI_TAGS=, =AI_EFFORT=,
  =AI_DEADLINE= or =AI_SCHEDULED=, =AI_IMPACT=, =AI_RELATED= (ids or
  WikiWords).  Trigger: =org-capture-after-finalize-hook= queues the
  entry, an idle timer decorates the queue; ",-o-c" decorates the whole
  inbox on demand.  The Inbox view gains a third super-agenda group
  "Decorated -- confirm" (=:property "AI_HASH"=) and three keys in
  =org-agenda-mode-map=: accept (applies every =AI_*= to its real
  field and refiles), edit (opens column view on the entry), reject
  (deletes the suggestions, leaves the entry byte-identical).  The
  Emacs brief confirms nothing published does this; the build is ours.
- After it exists, you type :: the raw line, and one key per item to
  accept.
- Test ::
  - a proposal names only candidates it was given (a refile target
    outside =org-refile-targets= is rejected by the core);
  - effort comes from the calibration table for the proposed category,
    not from the model's text;
  - a date phrase in the body ("by Friday", "next Tue") yields a
    =AI_DEADLINE= or =AI_SCHEDULED= equal to =org-read-date='s parse;
  - accepting writes the real fields and removes every =AI_*=;
    rejecting leaves the entry identical to its captured bytes;
  - an entry that already carries a real field never gets a suggestion
    for it (no overwrite);
  - a stubbed model that returns garbage produces no proposal and an
    error line, never a partial write.
- Must not :: no interactive prompts through emacsclient (decoration runs
  from a hook and a timer, never a minibuffer); verify in batch with a
  stubbed model; no colour beyond the ink ladder for the "confirm" group.
- Depends on :: gptel and irs (present); the migration only for refile
  candidates worth choosing among.

*** G15 The derived clock: intervals from state transitions  (rank 2; size M)
- Status [2026-09-09] :: *BUILT*, with three deliberate deviations from
  the mechanism below.  (1) The interval reader is not a new
  =org-transitions= package: it is =org-queue-state-log= /
  =org-queue-state-intervals= in =org-queue-harvest.el=, where the
  parser already lived, so the chart and the calibration cannot
  disagree about a duration.  (2) The consumers are a new zettapkg,
  =source/zettapkg/org-gantt/= -- a pure core with 23 batch tests, an
  org-ql harvest, an SVG Gantt with the clock table under it, and three
  read-only =org-timegrid= backends (spent, planned, composite).
  (3) Clipping is =org-gantt-window= rather than G6's table, and the
  overlap rule defaults to =wall= rather than =share=, because the
  queue already measures that way and switching would move every
  recorded calibration factor silently.  Not built: the
  =org-transitions-materialize= switch (no CLOCK lines are written
  anywhere) and =org-transitions-table= under that name -- the dynamic
  block is =org-gantt-table=.  An entry still in PROG from a previous
  day accrues every working hour since; that is kept and flagged
  =:provisional=, never capped.
- Delivers :: Work the block, Weekly review; C2, R7.  Nothing here uses
  =org-clock=.  Time is expressed the way Jira expresses it: as TODO
  state transitions, every one already logged with a timestamp into the
  LOGBOOK (=TODO(t!)=, =PROG(p!)=, =WAIT(w@/!)= ...).  The calibration, the
  capacity, the done list and the day log all want actuals, and the
  actuals are assembled from those transitions.  This is a build of your
  own; it keeps rank 2 because every number in R7 comes from it.
- Mechanism :: A zettapkg package =org-transitions= in org-queue's shape.
  =org-transitions-core.el= (pure) takes an entry's state-log lines as
  plists, =(:from "TODO" :to "PROG" :at 20260909.0912)=, and returns
  intervals.  The rule: an interval opens at a transition INTO =PROG=
  and closes at the next transition OUT of it, to any state; a note on a
  transition (the =@= keywords) changes nothing.  Intervals are clipped
  to the routine's blocks from G6 (a default weekday window until G6
  exists), so an entry left in =PROG= overnight accrues Tuesday's last
  hour and Wednesday's first two and never the night; that is the whole
  of "clock hygiene", and it needs no ritual.  When several entries are
  =PROG= at once (WIP allows three) the clipped minutes are shared equally
  among them; =org-transitions-overlap= chooses =share= (default), =wall=
  (each in full, the way Jira counts time in status) or =latest= (only
  the most recently started).  A transition straight from =TODO= to
  =DONE= is zero minutes and says so.  An interval in =AGENT= is machine
  time: kick to land, kept per chain, never clipped to the working window
  and never in a human total; in the built version that is a machine
  state class beside =org-gantt-working-states= (G16).  =org-transitions-harvest.el= reads
  the =State "PROG" from "TODO" [ts]= lines (the same lines
  =org-agenda-get-progress= parses for log mode) and the routine table.
  Consumers: =org-queue-harvest= fills =:clocked= from intervals instead
  of CLOCK lines (one function swap, =org-queue-harvest-actual-function=);
  the calibration, G2's done list, G3's time table and G9's day log read
  intervals.  Materialising =CLOCK:= lines into the drawer, so the stock
  clocktable and =CLOCKSUM= work unchanged, is a switch
  (=org-transitions-materialize=), off by default: it is lossy under
  overlap, since a CLOCK line cannot carry a share, and a derived line in
  a drawer can be mistaken for a typed one.  An =org-transitions-table=
  dynamic block with the clocktable's columns replaces it.
- After it exists, you type :: nothing beyond the state changes you
  already make: entering =PROG= is the start, leaving it is the stop.
- Test :: its own suite, plists in and plists out:
  - =TODO -> PROG [09:12]= then =PROG -> DONE [10:40]= yields one interval
    of 88 minutes;
  - =PROG -> WAIT= closes the interval exactly as =PROG -> DONE= does;
    =WAIT -> PROG= opens another; the entry's total is the sum;
  - a transition carrying a note parses to the same interval as one
    without;
  - =TODO -> DONE= yields zero minutes and no interval;
  - an interval from 16:00 Tuesday to 10:00 Wednesday, clipped to a
    template with blocks 08:00-12:00 and 13:30-17:00, yields 60 + 120
    minutes, with the raw span reported alongside;
  - two entries in =PROG= over the same 60 clipped minutes get 30 each
    under =share=, 60 each under =wall=, and 60 and 0 under =latest=;
  - an entry still in =PROG= at the pinned "now" yields an open interval
    ending at now, flagged =:open=; one still open from a previous day is
    flagged =:stale= with the day it was opened;
  - a habit entry's =DONE= transitions (a =.+= repeater flips it straight
    back to =TODO=) produce no interval;
  - =PROG -> AGENT [09:30]= then =AGENT -> NEXT [09:47]= yields a machine
    interval of 17 minutes on the chain and nothing on the human side;
  - materialising twice writes each CLOCK line once, and the sum of
    materialised minutes equals the =wall= total.
- Must not :: never read or write =CLOCK:= lines except under the
  materialise switch; never prompt; never alter a LOGBOOK state line.
- Depends on :: nothing to compute; G6 for honest clipping.  Every
  number in R7, the calibration in ",-o-C" and G11 depend on it.

*** G16 The agent chain: kickoff, flight, landing, review  (rank 3; size M)
- Delivers :: Chain; C2, C5, C8, R13, R14.  The first mechanism in the
  document with no ancestor in Part 1: most code is now written by an
  agent you kick off and review, ten to twenty minutes later, several
  at a time, and nothing in the config knows it is happening.
- Mechanism ::
  - One state.  =AGENT(a!)= inserted after =PROG= in the sequence: an
    agent has the ball.  Not =WAIT=: that state prompts for a note, is
    bucketed by days in the Chase view and is hidden from planning
    unless a deadline is near, all wrong for a delegation that returns
    in minutes and certainly needs you.  The lifecycle uses states you
    already have: =PROG= while you write the prompt; =AGENT= on kickoff;
    on return =NEXT= if the run finished (you committed to the review the
    moment you kicked it off, so "you decided this" holds) or =QUES= if
    the agent stopped to ask; =PROG= while you review; then =DONE=,
    =AGENT= again for the next iteration, or =NOPE=.  Every hop is a
    logged transition, so G15 gets the split for free: =PROG= intervals
    are human minutes, =AGENT= intervals are machine minutes, kick to
    land is latency, land to =PROG= is review lag, and =AGENT=
    transitions per chain is the iteration count.  In the G15 that was
    built this is one setting: =AGENT= goes in a new
    =org-gantt-machine-states= beside =org-gantt-working-states= (which
    must not list it), so =org-queue-state-intervals= keeps it out of
    every human total and the Gantt draws it as the chain's own lane,
    unclipped by =org-gantt-window= since agents run at night.
  - The prompt is the task.  The entry body is the prompt, or a
    sub-heading named =Prompt= when the body holds notes as well; the
    heading names the outcome.  Writing tasks is writing prompts, which
    is the one kind of writing C1 leaves to you.  A chain is the parent
    heading (a project in G8's sense); each iteration is a child, or the
    same entry re-kicked, the transition log telling which.
  - Kickoff (",-o-i", "in flight").  From the entry: refuse if there is
    no prompt or if =org-queue-chain-limit= (three) entries are already
    =AGENT= (=C-u= overrides, and the override is logged); generate a
    UUID into =:AGENT_SESSION:=; open a terminal in the repo
    (=:AGENT_REPO:=, proposed by G1 from the text, else asked once per
    chain) running =claude --session-id UUID "<prompt>"=, an interactive
    session with the prompt as its first turn (verify both against the
    installed CLI); transition to =AGENT=.  Overnight and complex runs
    use =C-u C-u=: the same command with =claude -p= in a git worktree,
    scheduled by =:KICK_AT:= and fired by a timer, because an unattended
    session cannot be interactive.  The prompt text is never altered by
    the command.
  - Landing.  The Claude Code =Stop= hook already runs =zetta-notify=;
    it also appends a plist to =.data/org/agent-landings.el=:
    =(:session UUID :at TS :cwd DIR :event stop)=.  The =Notification=
    hook (permission or idle prompt) appends =:event question= with the
    text.  Emacs watches the file (=file-notify-add-watch=, a timer as
    fallback) and =zetta-org-agent-land= applies each new line: find the
    entry by =:AGENT_SESSION:=, transition =AGENT= to =NEXT= (a =stop=) or
    to =QUES= (a =question=, with =org-inhibit-logging= bound to =note=,
    which keeps the timestamp and skips the =@= prompt; verify, and the
    question written under the Prompt heading); nothing else is stamped,
    since the transition carries the time.  A line for a session no
    entry knows is reported as an orphan, never dropped.  No
    =emacsclient --eval= from the hook, and no minibuffer anywhere in
    the path.
  - Views.  The Day view gains three groups: "Landed -- review" (=NEXT=
    whose last transition was from =AGENT=, a =:pred= over the LOGBOOK
    like the existing ones) at the top beside =NEXT=; "In flight"
    (=AGENT=, with kick time and elapsed minutes in the prefix); "Agent
    asks" (=QUES= carrying =:AGENT_SESSION:=).  A Chains view (",-o-v a")
    lists each chain with iterations, latency, review lag and its next
    prompt or the absence of one.
  - The queue.  =AGENT= is excluded from candidates with reason
    =in-flight= and never counts against human capacity.  Landed entries
    are commitments already (=NEXT=); their planned slice is the review
    share of =:Effort:= (=org-queue-review-share=, start at one half),
    since an agent task's effort means your minutes per cycle, spec plus
    review.  The morning report adds one line: chains in flight, the
    landings expected today from G15's measured latency and your
    measured cycle, and the review minutes they imply; when that exceeds
    a block the plan says so, which is the intake gate for chains.
  - Windows, from G6.  Kick windows: block starts, before the lift, the
    close.  Review windows: pomodoro breaks and block ends.  Quiet
    windows: deep units, during which =zetta-notify= holds every landing
    except a question, which shows in the mode line and nothing more.
  - Priming, in G2.  The close lists each active chain with or without
    a next prompt; you write the missing ones and mark the overnight
    ones; the morning kicks the rest with one key.
- After it exists, you type :: the prompt, the review verdict, and one
  key to kick.
- Test ::
  - a landing line whose session matches an =AGENT= entry transitions it
    to =NEXT= on =stop= and to =QUES= on =question=, once; a re-read of the
    log applies nothing twice;
  - a line whose session matches no entry, or an entry not in =AGENT=,
    is reported as an orphan and changes no state;
  - the fourth kick with three in flight is refused; with =C-u= it is
    applied and the override is logged;
  - the prompt is the =Prompt= sub-heading when one exists, else the
    body, verbatim; an entry with neither refuses to kick;
  - from a transition fixture, latency, review lag and iterations per
    chain come out as the known numbers, and =AGENT= minutes never
    appear in a human total;
  - the expected-landings line for three chains, at a measured latency
    of fifteen minutes and a twenty-minute cycle of your own, over a
    four-hour block reports review minutes that exceed the block; for
    two chains it does not;
  - a =:KICK_AT:= before dawn fires the headless mode only; an
    interactive entry with a =:KICK_AT:= is refused at scheduling time;
  - the "landed" predicate is true for =NEXT= from =AGENT= and false for
    =NEXT= from =TODO=.
- Must not :: no hook may evaluate elisp in the daemon; no landing may
  raise a prompt; no notification outside =zetta-notify=; the kick never
  edits the prompt; the fourth chain is refused, not queued silently;
  headless mode is never the default.
- Depends on :: G15, already built as =org-queue-state-intervals= and
  =org-gantt=, for the metrics and the split; G6 for the windows; G2 for
  priming; G1 for =kind: delegate= and the repo proposal.

*** G6 The routine as a machine-readable template  (rank 4; size S)

*** G2 Close the day  (rank 5; size S)

** 3.5 Order of adoption

One stage at a time, Zen to Done's rule; each stage runs at least a week
before the next, and the existing plan's first step comes first.

- Stage 0 (now; the existing plan) :: Migrate =(todo) emacs.org= with
  =zetta-org-migrate-checkboxes=, live with it a week; build G15's
  transition clock (your own build, the first pure core after org-queue)
  and work normally by state transitions for a fortnight; read
  =org-queue-capacity= off the intervals.  Stops by hand: nothing yet;
  this is the data every later stage reads.
- Stage 1 (the day's shape) :: G6 template, G2 close, G16 agent chain.
  Stops by hand: guessing capacity; re-deciding carry-over each morning;
  remembering tomorrow's fixed blocks; remembering what is in flight and
  when to look; the day is closed by a key and tomorrow's agents are
  primed by it.
- Stage 2 (clarify) :: G1 decoration, G7 duplicates and "still worth
  it?".  Stops by hand: typing state, tags, effort, refile target and
  dates on inbox items; grooming the parked pile; the inbox is emptied by
  accept keys.
- Stage 3 (the week) :: G3 pack, G8 invariant, G13 calendar.  Stops by
  hand: assembling eight agenda views on Monday; typing appointments;
  hunting for projects that quietly stopped.
- Stage 4 (the body) :: G4 habits, G14 timer.  Stops by hand: keeping
  the routine in your head; deciding when to ring a timer; the routine
  file becomes the record.
- Stage 5 (the season) :: G5 horizons and area floors, G12 intake, G10
  promote and resurface.  Stops by hand: judging whether work serves
  anything; the yes to a new deadline is answered with evidence; notes
  become pages with a reason.
- Stage 6 (evidence) :: G13 mail and git, G9 day log and the date button,
  G11 backpressure.  Stops by hand: writing waiting-fors; asking "what did
  I do on Tuesday"; spreading a long task before its deadline.

The test of each stage is the one the plan already uses: after a week,
would going back to the checkbox list be a relief?  If yes, the stage
added bookkeeping and is reverted before the next begins.


** 3.6 The AI decoration contract

*** What decoration is, and is not

Decoration is classification of a raw inbox entry against lists that
already exist in the config, plus extraction of what the text says
(dates, people, links), plus retrieval of what the kb already holds about
it (neighbours, duplicates).  It is the composite's rule 11 applied: the
person writes the words and decides commitment or option; the model
sorts against a dozen known labels and fetches what search can fetch.
The knowledge cluster found that across every note system the human
hand-writes five things, and three of them are classification against a
small known list (project-versus-area, the vocabulary tag, the recall
prompt).  Those three are the job.  The own-words restatement and the
reason for a link are not, and neither is the decision that a thing is a
commitment.

Invariants, each of which is a test in G1:

- Proposes, never commits.  Decoration writes only =AI_=-prefixed
  properties.  It never sets the TODO keyword, tags, =SCHEDULED=,
  =DEADLINE=, =Effort=, =IMPACT= or any canonical property, and never
  refiles.  Promotion is a separate function that runs only on a
  keystroke.
- Never alters the raw text.  Heading and body are read, hashed, and left
  byte-identical.  The hash is part of the idempotence key.
- Never proposes =NEXT=, =PROG=, =DONE= or =NOPE=.  Those are decisions
  (a person chose it; a person started it; a person finished or refused
  it).  The model may propose =TODO=, =WAIT=, =QUES=, =HOLD=, =IDEA=, or
  no keyword (a note).
- At most one context tag and at most one energy tag, both from the
  =org-tag-alist= groups; any other proposed tag must already exist in
  the corpus (=org-global-tags-completion-table= over the agenda files).
- Only values from the closed lists.  A category not in the agenda
  files' =#+CATEGORY= set, a keyword not in =org-todo-keywords-1=, an
  effort not in =Effort_ALL=, an impact outside 1-5, a deadline type
  other than hard/soft: dropped, with the drop recorded in =AI_WHY=.
- Reversible.  =zetta-decorate-strip= removes every =AI_= property and
  nothing else; promotion records what it wrote so it can be undone by
  =undo= in the buffer (one =atomic-change-group=).
- Idempotent.  Re-running over a decorated entry whose content hash,
  prompt version and schema version are unchanged does nothing.
- Stamped.  =AI_STAMP= carries the timestamp, backend and model name,
  prompt version and schema version; =AI_CONFIDENCE= carries an overall
  score and per-field scores.

*** Inputs

The raw entry:

- Heading text, body text (everything under the heading to the next
  heading, minus drawers), the =%a= backlink if the =N= or =m= template
  wrote one, =:CREATED:= (every template writes it), and which template
  made the entry when that is recoverable (=m= entries land in
  =(todo) email.org= and carry a =mu4e:= link; =t= entries already have
  =TODO=, tags and =Effort=, so decoration only fills gaps).
- The source kind from the backlink type: =mu4e:= mail, =elfeed:= or
  https from elfeed, =eww:=/https web, =file:= a file buffer, =info:=,
  =docview:=/pdf.  A voicenote arrives through readwise as an org file in
  =~/kb/readwise/voicenotes/=, not through org-capture; it is decorated
  when copied into the inbox (see "Where it runs").

The model receives the entry, the lists above rendered as compact
enumerations with their one-line semantics, the resolved dates
(computed in elisp, not by the model: see "Failure modes"), and the irs
hits as titles plus paths.  It does not receive the rest of the inbox
or any other note body.

*** Outputs

Storage: =AI_=-prefixed properties in the entry's ordinary
=:PROPERTIES:= drawer, not a second drawer.  Org recognises one property
drawer per entry; a custom =:DECORATION:= drawer would be invisible to
=org-entry-get=, to =org-ql='s =property= predicate, to column view and to
=org-refile=, so the Inbox view could not group "decorated / not yet",
the promotion code would need its own parser, and a refile would leave
the proposals behind.  Prefixed properties are queryable, travel with the
entry, cannot collide with canonical names (no canonical property starts
with =AI_=), and are hidden from the drawer display by adding the prefix
to =org-custom-properties= (verify that it takes a regexp; if not, list
the names).

*** Where it runs

Three triggers, and the recommendation is to use two of them:

- (c) Asynchronously on =org-capture-after-finalize-hook=, for the entry
  just captured.  Capture stays instant because =gptel-request= returns
  immediately and the callback writes the properties when the answer
  arrives; the hook already carries =zetta-popup-frame-delete=, so the
  pattern is established.  The callback must locate the entry by =:ID:=
  or by =CREATED= plus heading, not by position, since the inbox is
  =:prepend t= and a second capture can land above it before the reply.
- (b) On demand from the Inbox view, over every undecorated entry
  (=AI_HASH= absent or stale).  This is the catch for entries that never
  passed through org-capture on this machine: phone edits through
  Syncthing, voicenotes copied in from readwise, entries captured on the
  hub.  One command, =zetta-decorate-inbox=, with a progress message and a
  =zetta-notify= when the batch lands.
- (a) A timer or the hub.  Not recommended.  The closed lists live in
  Emacs state (=org-todo-keywords=, =org-tag-alist=, the agenda files'
  categories, the org-ql project list, the HyWiki word list); a script on
  the hub would need a second copy of all of it, and the hub has no irs.

LLM path.  =gptel-request= with =:schema= and =:callback=, in Emacs, not
a Python script.  Reasons: the lists are Emacs state and re-exporting
them is a second source of truth; gptel already has the backends and the
irs tools; the callback writes with =org-entry-put= in the entry's own
buffer so =undo= works and the file's own hooks and logging apply; the
work splits cleanly into a pure merge core (testable in batch with
canned proposals) and a thin request shell.  =:schema= takes a JSON
schema or gptel's shorthand and asks the backend for structured output;
=gptel--parse-schema= is a generic dispatched per backend, so check that
the chosen backend implements it (the Anthropic and OpenAI-compatible
backends do; verify Ollama through the OpenAI-compatible endpoint).

Backend.  Default to the existing Claude backend for quality on the
classification and the date phrases, with two provisions.  The entries
are short, so cost is negligible: forty entries with the lists rendered
is on the order of a hundred thousand input tokens once, and one entry at
a time thereafter.  Privacy: the inbox is personal and every decorated
entry leaves the machine in full.  So (1) an entry tagged =:private:= or
captured under a template that sets =AI_SKIP= is never sent, and (2) a
=zetta-decorate-backend= variable can point at the local Ollama
=qwen2.5:7b= that the router stack already uses for classification, which
supports structured output and keeps everything on the machine at the
cost of precision.  Run the G1 precision harness against both and pick
per field: local for the enumerations if it clears the threshold, hosted
for the rest.  The router proxy on :8765 is not a dependency; it was
down on 2026-08-14 and adds nothing here.

*** The confirm gesture

In the Inbox view (=,-o-v i=), which already splits "Not yet a task" from
"A task, refile it", add a third grouping by decoration state:
"Proposed" (has =AI_HASH=, not promoted), "Undecorated", "Promoted"
(should be empty, since promotion refiles).  Each proposed line shows the
proposal inline in the agenda prefix: keyword, target, context, effort
(=org-agenda-prefix-format= can call a function; verify the =%(...)=
form).

Keys, on the =org-agenda-bulk-custom-functions= mechanism so that they
work on marked items and on the item at point alike (verify that
=org-agenda-bulk-action= falls back to the item at point when nothing is
marked; if it does not, bind the same functions on single-item keys):

- accept all :: promote every proposal on the entry: set the keyword
  with =org-todo= (so the state log fires), set tags with
  =org-set-tags=, write =Effort=, =IMPACT=, =DEADLINE_TYPE=,
  =WAITING_ON=; write =SCHEDULED=/=DEADLINE= with =org-schedule= and
  =org-deadline= (so the initial-deadline logging fires); write a plain
  timestamp for an event; append =AI_RELATED= links to the body as a
  "Related:" line; strip the =AI_= properties; refile with =org-refile=
  to =AI_TARGET= (=org-refile-use-outline-path 'file= makes the flat file
  a valid target).  One keystroke.
- accept one field :: a prompt over the proposed fields; promotes that
  field only.
- edit :: opens the entry with the proposals shown as the default values
  in the same prompts capture uses (=org-set-effort= offers =Effort_ALL=,
  =org-set-tags-command= offers the groups).
- reject :: strip the proposals and mark =AI_REJECTED= with the stamp, so
  the batch command does not re-decorate it under the same prompt
  version.
- skip :: leave as is.

Every correction is a training example.  When a promoted value differs
from the proposal, or a field is rejected, append a plist to
=.data/org/decoration-corrections.el= (next to
=org-queue-history-file=, unsynced and uncommitted because it contains
inbox text): =(:hash H :field kind :proposed "note" :corrected "task"
:prompt 3 :stamp T)=.  The G1 harness reads this file as extra fixture
cases, so the prompt is improved from evidence rather than from
impression, and the corrections file's growth rate is itself a metric
in the review pack.

The gesture is confirm or correct; nothing else.  The common case (a
bare note that is obviously a shopping item) is one keystroke.  The
uncommon case (a question that should become a project) is edit, and
the edit prompts are the ones already in use.

*** Failure modes and guards

- Hallucinated category or target.  Validated in the core against the
  lists before anything is written; an unknown value is dropped, the
  drop is written to =AI_WHY=, and the field's confidence is zero.  The
  model never sees a free-text category field; the schema is an enum.
- Over-tagging.  The schema allows one context and one energy; topical
  tags must exist already; more than three topical tags is rejected.
- False duplicates.  Worse than a miss, because a false one hides a real
  item.  A duplicate needs two signals: an irs score above the threshold
  and either an exact normalised-text match or the same backlink.  A
  single-signal duplicate is reported as related, not as duplicate.
- Dates.  The model returns the phrase ("Thursday at 2pm", "tue", "end of
  month"), not the date.  Elisp resolves it with =org-read-date= against
  =CREATED= as the base time, not against now: "tue" written on Thursday
  the 3rd means the 8th, whatever day decoration runs.  If the resolved
  date is already past at decoration time, the proposal is kept and
  marked =AI_DATE_STALE=, and the entry is grouped separately in the
  Inbox view.
- The two-minute class.  Decoration proposes =AI_EFFORT: 0:05= and
  =AI_KIND: task=; it does not do the task and does not suggest doing it
  now.  The Inbox view can group "under five minutes" so the person can
  batch them, which is Cirillo's "add it up".
- Already decorated.  The idempotence key is (=AI_HASH=, prompt version,
  schema version).  A changed body re-decorates; a promoted or rejected
  field is never overwritten by a re-run (corrections win).
- Model drift.  The stamp carries backend, model, prompt and schema
  versions; the G1 precision harness is the regression suite and is run
  whenever any of the three changes.
- The inbox grows because decorated feels done.  Decoration is not
  processing.  The review pack reports the inbox count and its age
  distribution, and the Inbox view's header shows both.  The queue never
  harvests the inbox, so nothing in it can be planned until promoted.
- The reply arrives after the entry moved.  The callback finds the entry
  by =:ID:= (write one at capture with =org-id-get-create=; the fixture
  lesson from the smart tree applies: the real kb has no IDs, so the
  capture templates must add them from now on) and falls back to
  =CREATED= plus heading; if neither matches, the proposal is written to
  the corrections file as an orphan and reported.

Which feeds are free, which need a hook, which need a source, and the
order to build them.

Free, already logged: CREATED, last touch, carry-over, the state
transitions (G15 turns them into intervals, and until then there are no
actuals), the done list, appointments, backlinks, on-this-day, the git
timeline.  These need readers, not writers; every one is a pure function
over data that exists, which is why the review pack (G3) and
close-the-day (G2) come first: they are assembled almost entirely from
these.

One hook each: interruptions (record the current =PROG= entry into the
capture's properties at finalize), habit ticks (the =DONE= transition on
the routine entry), times surfaced (a counter incremented by
the view that shows a parked item), landings (the Stop and Notification
hooks appending to the landing log, G16), duplicates and related (the
decoration callback).  Cheap, and each unlocks one composite ritual:
interruptions make Cirillo's count real, ticks make the strength score
real, surfacings make dismissal real.

External sources: WAITING_ON from sent mail (mu4e is present and indexed,
so this is a query and a parser, but it is the one feed that reads
another program's store), commits to journal lines (the repos are on
disk; the mapping from repo to project is the only new fact), capacity
from the template (needs the template made machine-readable first, G6).

Order: G15's transition clock first, because every actual comes from
it and it is your own build; G6 next, because capacity is the number
every plan depends on and it is currently a labelled guess; then G16,
because most of the working day now runs through it and its sensor
already fires; then G3 and G2, because
they are readers over free data and they are the two rituals the
composite adds; then the decoration hook (G1), because it is the largest
bookkeeping reducer and it is independent of the rest; then the hooks
(G9, G4, G7); then the external feeds (G13); backpressure (G11) and the
intake gate (G12) last, since both are refinements of a queue that is
already trusted.

** 3.8 The test catalogue

The rule is the one =org-queue= established: the arithmetic lives in a
core file that takes plists and returns plists, tested in =emacs -Q
--batch= with no Org files; the Org shell that reads files and draws
buffers is thin and is not tested in batch.  Views are checked by
looking at them.  The tests are here because the repo builds this way,
not because the ask said so: your writing is tasks, and these suites
exist so that the mechanisms which take writing away from you can be
trusted without being watched.  Day to day they are run, not written.
Each suite below names the fixture, the cases, the invariants, and what
is deliberately left to the eye.

*** G1 decoration

Fixture: =test/fixtures/inbox.org=, thirty to forty entries shaped like
the real inbox (bare notes, questions, mail captures with =mu4e:= links,
elfeed captures, a voicenote transcript, two near-duplicates, one exact
duplicate, entries with relative dates written on known weekdays, one
entry already carrying =TODO= and =Effort= from the =t= template, one
tagged =:private:=), each with an =:ID:=; =expected.eld=, a plist per
entry with the expected values per field and a tolerance for effort
(one rung).  A second fixture, =proposals.eld=, holds canned model
outputs, some deliberately wrong.

Batch cases (no LLM, no Org):
- =org-decorate-core-validate=: an unknown category is dropped and
  reported; =NEXT=, =PROG=, =DONE=, =NOPE= are rejected; two context tags
  keep the higher-confidence one and flag it; a topical tag not in the
  corpus list is dropped; effort outside =Effort_ALL= is dropped; impact
  without evidence is dropped; a deadline type other than hard/soft is
  dropped.
- =org-decorate-core-resolve-date=: "tue" with CREATED Thursday
  2026-09-03 resolves to 2026-09-08; "tomorrow" to 2026-09-04; "end of
  month" to 2026-09-30; "next week" to Monday 2026-09-07 (verify against
  =org-read-date='s own reading and pin whichever it gives); a phrase that
  does not parse yields nil with a reason; a resolved date earlier than
  the decoration time is marked stale, not dropped.
- =org-decorate-core-merge=: given an entry plist with canonical
  =:todo "TODO"= and a proposal =keyword "QUES"=, the merge plan writes
  =AI_KEYWORD= and never touches =:todo=; a canonical =Effort= present
  means =AI_EFFORT= is still recorded but marked =canonical-present=;
  the plan lists property writes only, never a heading edit.
- Idempotence: same hash, same prompt and schema version yields an empty
  plan; a changed hash yields a full plan; a corrected field (from the
  corrections fixture) survives a re-run.
- Duplicates: exact normalised text is a duplicate; irs score 0.9 with
  no second signal is related, not duplicate; score 0.9 plus the same
  backlink is a duplicate.
- The =:private:= entry yields a plan that sends nothing.

Invariants: every plan is a list of =AI_= property writes and nothing
else; validation is total (no input raises); resolve-date never reads
the wall clock; the merge never emits a canonical property name.

Not in batch: the precision harness.  =make decorate-eval= runs the real
backend over the fixture, scores per field (kind at or above 0.9,
keyword 0.85, target 0.8, context 0.75, effort within a rung 0.7, dates
0.9, duplicate precision 0.9), prints a table, and is run by hand when
the prompt, schema, backend or corrections file changes.  It needs a
key and a network, so it stays out of CI.

*** G2 close-the-day

Fixture: a corpus plist set (reuse =testdata/todo= through the harvest,
or hand-built plists with a pinned today), a plan-history plist for
today, a list of state-change events with timestamps, the =PROG=

Cases: the checklist plist names the inbox count, undecorated count,
overdue commitments, unfiled promoted entries, tomorrow's appointments;

date); it never mutates its inputs; every planned task ends the close in

Not in batch: the buffer, the notification, the phrase.

*** G4 habits

Fixture: hand-built series plists: =(:habit "lift" :frequency 1 :days
((20260901 . done) (20260902 . done) ...))=, including runs of thirty,
sixty and ninety days, a single miss, two consecutive misses, an
explicit skip, and a three-times-a-week habit.

Cases: with =m = 0.5^(sqrt(frequency)/13)=, thirty consecutive days
from zero give a score within 0.005 of 0.798, sixty give 0.959, ninety
give 0.992 (these are the brief's numbers and the closed-form
=1 - m^n=); one miss after thirty multiplies the score by m and raises
no alert; two consecutive misses raise =:alert two-misses=; a skip
leaves the score unchanged and does not count as a miss; the repair
window: a done within one day of a miss clears the pending alert.  For
the three-a-week habit verify Loop's definition of frequency for
non-daily habits before pinning numbers; until then test only that its
half-life is longer than the daily one.  Habits never appear among the
queue's candidates: given a corpus where three entries carry =:habit t=,
=org-queue-core-plan= returns them in =:dropped= with reason =habit=,
never in =:planned= or =:cut=.  Fixed habit minutes per weekday are
subtracted from capacity before slack: a Monday with 300 discretionary
minutes and 90 minutes of anchored habits packs against 210 times 0.8.

Invariants: the score is in [0, 1]; the series is append-only; a habit
has no deadline and no overdue state (the plist rejects them).

Not in batch: the chart.

state changes and captures in time order with ties broken by kind; gaps
over fifteen minutes with nothing in =PROG= inside a focus block are

with a mail or chat backlink).

Invariants: the log is sorted; every event appears once.

Not in batch: the =j= capture template and its =%(...)= expansion.

*** G11 start-by and backpressure

Fixture: tasks with deadline, effort and calibration factor; per-day
discretionary capacity from G6.

Cases: start-by equals deadline minus the days needed at the daily

the deadline; when the window cannot hold the effort the result is an
overcommitment reason naming the shortfall in minutes; a soft deadline
spreads with the soft factor's weight and never displaces hard work.

Invariants: the spread is deterministic; a task is never split below
the unit (start at 30 minutes).

*** G12 intake gate

Fixture: a corpus with committed efforts across a fortnight, capacity

Cases: the gate reports hours already committed on or before the date,

commitment that overflows the window returns the overflow in minutes

Invariants: the gate reads the same corpus the queue packs, so the two

Not in batch: the prompt at deadline-setting time (an advice on

* Part 5 — Open questions, and decisions that are yours

Ordered by how much of Part 3 waits on the answer.

1. *The routine contradicts its own principle.*  schedule.org says
   "structure (pomodoros) is placed where focus is weakest", then puts
   pomodoros in the first two hours of block one, "while focus is fresh".
   The composite follows the principle (rule 10: pomodoros in the dip,
   the deep block never rung).  If the first-two-hours pomodoros are
   deliberate, say so and G14 gets a second window; if not, the routine
   should be corrected before G6 parses it.
2. *Where habits live.*  Either a =(todo) routine.org= inside the agenda
   files with =:STYLE: habit= (the consistency graph appears in the agenda,
   and the queue must exclude the style) or a =habits.org= outside them
   (nothing to exclude, no graph in the agenda, a chart of its own).
   Part 3's G4 recommends one; the choice is yours because it decides
   whether the lift and the bike show in the Day view at all.
3. *Is there a calendar to bridge?*  Nothing in the kb shows one.
   Appointments today are plain timestamps in =(todo) calendar.org=.  If
   an external calendar exists, org-caldav or khalel (Appendix A) turns
   it into derived evidence; if not, G13's calendar feed is moot and the
   timestamps stay the hard landscape.
4. *Which LLM runs decoration, and where.*  The inbox is personal.  Part
   3.6 recommends a path; the privacy line (what leaves the machine) is a
   decision, not a default.  A local model through gptel is the
   conservative answer and is probably good enough for classification
   against a dozen labels.
5. *Is =PROG= the pick?*  Bernt Hansen lets the act of starting (for him,
   clocking in) promote a task to NEXT, so the day's commitment is
   derived from what you actually began, which fits C2.  Here the act of
   starting is the transition into =PROG=, and the queue reads =NEXT= as a
   commitment made in advance by a person, which fits C5.  Either =NEXT=
   stays a prior decision and =PROG= only earns the progress boost, or a
   transition into =PROG= retroactively counts as a pick.  One or the
   other, because both at once would let a stray start commit the day.
6. *Dependencies: =:BLOCKED_BY:= or org-edna.*  time-management.org chose
   the property for testability.  org-edna adds sequential projects that
   advance themselves and a blocker hook the packer can respect.  Revisit
   only if the property proves too weak; the composite does not need it.
7. *Dismissal: automatic or proposed.*  After N surfacings without a pull,
   an option can be marked dismissed by the system or merely proposed for
   dismissal in the weekly pack.  Forster dismisses automatically;
   C5 says nothing is silently dropped.  Part 3.2's G7 proposes; the
   automatic version is a =setq= away if the pack becomes noise.
8. *What this season's theme is.*  The horizons file cannot be seeded by
   anyone else.  One sentence, and at most three missions.
9. *Capacity.*  Still a guess (five hours on a weekday).  The plan already
   says work normally for a fortnight and read the number off G15's
   intervals; the routine's two focus blocks
   suggest the real number is nearer seven and a half, minus the dip.
10. *The permanent layer.*  HyWiki pages are the composite's permanent
    notes; =~/kb/notes= holds area and resource documents.  Whether a note
    like diet.org is an area (a standard) or a resource (an interest)
    decides which review touches it.  A =#+filetags:= line per note is the
    cheapest way to say.
11. *How concurrent =PROG= entries share time.*  With three in progress
    at once, G15 must attribute a block's minutes: equally (the default),
    in full to each (Jira's way, which double-counts without apology), or
    to the most recently started only.  Calibration cannot live with
    double counting.  Pick one; the tests pin it.
12. *Two chains or three.*  The arithmetic at your numbers (ten to twenty
    minute runs, about twenty minutes of your own per cycle) saturates
    you at two chains; three is the ceiling for half-hour runs.  G16
    starts the limit at three and the morning report says when two
    would have done.  A limit that never binds is not a limit.
13. *Whether an agent's question may interrupt.*  A question idles its
    chain until answered; a landing does not.  G16 lets the question
    through quietly (mode line, no sound) and holds everything else for
    the unit boundary.  If the flag proves as distracting as a sound,
    hold questions too and accept the idle minutes.
14. *Where the prompt lives in the entry.*  The body is the prompt by
    default, a =Prompt= sub-heading when the entry also holds notes.
    Decide whether every delegated entry gets the sub-heading, which
    makes the kick command simpler and the entries uniform.

* Appendix A — Prior art in org-mode and Hyperbole

Condensed from the seventh research brief, which read package sources,
the Org 9.8.7 and Hyperbole 9.0.2pre manuals, and the practitioners' own
published configs on 2026-09-09.  "Last activity" is the last commit on
the default branch.  Where the brief could not confirm something it
says "unverified", and so does this.

** What the practitioners did, and what transfers

- *Bernt Hansen* (GTD-lite on continuous clocking).  Clocking in is the
  act that marks "current": =org-clock-in-switch-to-state= promotes a TODO
  leaf to NEXT, and a hook demotes any NEXT parent, so NEXT lives on
  leaves only.  Interruptions are captured as clocked, done-side
  =PHONE=/=MEETING= entries.  A "stuck project" is one with no NEXT child
  not tagged WAITING.  Transferable here, where no clock runs and
  transitions are the record: the idea that the act of starting can be
  the pick (Part 5, question 5), interruption capture that keeps the
  time story intact, and =org-todo-state-tags-triggers=.
- *Nicolas Rougier* (GTD by the book, four files).  Writes an =ACTIVATED=
  property the first time a task enters NEXT: a derived "started" stamp,
  sibling of this config's =:CREATED:=.  A "Completed today" agenda block
  from =CLOSED>="<today>"=.  Transferable: the ACTIVATED stamp.
- *Karl Voit* (no named system, no recurring reviews).  =:CREATED:= on
  every heading since 2011; categories, not files, split business from
  personal; someday is a tag plus the removal of SCHEDULED, not a state;
  agenda hygiene views as org-ql blocks ("unfinished, neither someday nor
  scheduled, so it probably needs someday").  Memacs (below) is his.
  Transferable: the hygiene views; the stance that most metadata should
  be generated.
- *Sacha Chua* (reviews generated from org).  The weekly review is
  assembled by a function from the agenda's log mode, her journal and a
  time table with last week's percentages; a =LAST_REVIEW= property lets
  missing weeks be generated in a loop.  Transferable: the whole idea of
  Part 3's review pack.
- *Rainer König* (GTD, the weekly review as a recurring task).  Counts
  =org-log-reschedule= lines into a =ROTTEN= property and views
  =ROTTEN>10=; this config already logs reschedules, so the counter is a
  derived metric waiting to be computed.  Transferable: the ROTTEN
  count as the "times carried" evidence in G7.
- *Protesilaos Stavrou* (Denote; tasks apart from notes).  Metadata in
  the file name; one persistent file per person for meetings; a
  =:CAPTURED:= stamp and =APPT_WARNTIME= for appointments.  Transferable:
  little here beyond the person-file idea, which HyRolo already serves.
- *Jethro Kuan* (org-roam).  Explicitly does not use dailies ("more noise
  than signal") and keeps a single inbox file.  Transferable: the
  confirmation that inbox-first survives contact with a Zettelkasten.
- *Bullet Journal in org*: no package exists.  Adrià López's migration
  flips carried originals to =POSTPONED= so migrations leave an audit
  trail, which is the one BuJo mechanic that maps onto the queue's
  carry-over count.
- *PARA in org*: one running implementation (=vulpea-para=, 2026), bound to
  the vulpea database; reclassification is a tag change, not a move.
  Nothing plain-org.
- *Newport in org*: Blaine Mooers' weekly-template generators and paper
  block sheets; org-timeblock; nothing for the shutdown ritual or
  multi-scale planning beyond König's review phases.

  Koutline and outline files with no index; its date model is one
  modification stamp per record (=hyrolo-date-format=), not a date
  index.  =hyrolo-fgrep= takes logical queries (=and=, =or=, =xor=, =not=,
  =r-and= ...).  The manual never suggests it as a to-do list; the
  join between the rolo corpus and todo state is =hyrolo-tags-view=.
  Ramin Honary's EmacsConf 2022 talk makes the Zettelkasten argument for
  HyRolo: a flat-file, no-database idea store whose links are Hyperbole
  buttons.
- HyWiki: WikiWords need no markup and can point at things other than
  pages (a file, a command, a key series, an Org ID, a HyRolo search,
  a glossary entry).  There is no stored backlink index;
  =hywiki-consult-backlink= greps.  The =hywiki-graph= package in this
  repo is the only graph view; upstream has none.
- The Koutliner gives every cell a permanent idstamp, a creator and a
  create-time, and klinks that survive moves; Matthew Jorgensen's 2023
  talk uses it for stream-of-thought journaling because Org "causes
  decision fatigue" in that mode.
- Implicit buttons: a plain path, URL, Org ID, key series, git commit
  reference or WikiWord is a link with no markup; =defal= and =defil=
  define a new button type in one line.
- *There is no date button type.*  A bare =2026-09-09= in a text file is
  not navigable in Hyperbole as shipped.  A =defil= over the ISO form and
  one over the =[[Sep 9th, 2026]]= journal form, both calling
  =zetta-hyrolo-day=, would make every plain-text date in any buffer a
  link to that day's evidence: the closest thing in this stack to
  Logseq's date pages without a database.

** Derived, not typed: the tools

- *Memacs* (Karl Voit; 2026-05, actively built): twenty-seven modules
  turning git commits, mail (with a =--delegation= flag that makes
  FLAGGED mail into NEXT/WAITING entries), ISO-dated filenames, browser
  history, calendars, phone logs and more into dated org headlines in
  =*.org_archive= files, read on demand with =org-agenda-archives-mode=.
  The canonical "what did I do that day" layer, and the one maintained
  commit-to-org tool.
- Nothing turns commits into time intervals or DONE entries; nothing
  exports ActivityWatch to org (the reverse direction exists, and it is
  clock-based).  Both are builds, and so is the transition clock itself:
  the survey found no package that turns the =State= log lines into
  time-in-state intervals, though it did not look for one specifically,
  and a search is cheap before building.  org-habit reads the same lines
  for its graph and =org-agenda-get-progress= parses them for log mode;
  neither sums them.
- No auto-tagging or effort-prediction package exists; the queue's
  calibration has no published competitor.
- *LLM triage of an org inbox does not exist as a package.*  Checked
  against MELPA, GitHub repo and code search, and every LLM package
  README (org-ai, ellama, gptel and gptel-agent, gptel-org-utils, ogent,
  khoj, elisa, org-workspace, ingest).  The nearest fragment is
  =gptel-org-utils-summarize=, which rewrites a headline from its body.
  Part 3.6's contract is therefore a build, and gptel is its substrate.

** Where the queue is already ahead

No published package scores a backlog and packs it into a day, calibrates
estimates against actuals per category with shrinkage (today it reads
CLOCK lines; G15 hands it intervals instead), models
carry-over as a score term, penalises category glut, or reports what it
cut and why.  Its nearest relatives are outside the task packages:
=org-habit-stats= (a derived score written back as properties) and Memacs
(evidence as headlines).  Two things worth bolting on rather than
rewriting: exclude =STYLE=habit= and blocked entries from candidates, and
expose the score as an =org-ql-defpred=.

  "Not yet a task -- decide what it is" (no keyword) / "A task -- refile it".

** Schema (what an entry can carry)
- States: TODO(t!) NEXT(N!) PROG(p!) WAIT(w@/!) QUES(q@/!) HOLD(h@/!) IDEA(i!)
  | DONE(d!) NOPE(n!).  Leaving into WAIT/QUES/HOLD prompts for a NOTE (why). |
  NEXT = "you decided this": the one human override; the queue treats it as
  a commitment, not a bonus.  IDEA = someday/maybe.  HOLD = paused by choice.
  QUES = open question (pairs with the org-remark question pen).
- Priority cookie A/B/C; ABSENT is a real value (scored between B and C).
- Properties: Effort (Effort_ALL 0 0:05 ... 7:00), IMPACT (1-5, 3 neutral;
  importance separated from urgency), DEADLINE_TYPE (hard|soft; soft = 0.35x
  urgency), CREATED (every capture), WAITING_ON (free-text person),
  REVIEW_ON (tickler date for HOLD/IDEA), BLOCKED_BY (an ID), ID.
- Tags: context group, mutually exclusive: @deep @shallow @errand @call
  @meeting @travel @social; energy group: @fresh @tired; topical tags free.
- Dates: SCHEDULED ("on/after"), DEADLINE ("by"), plain active timestamp =
  appointment (repeaters rolled forward to the next occurrence).
- Logging: everything into LOGBOOK drawers -- state changes, done, refile,
  reschedule, redeadline, initial schedule/deadline.  No CLOCK lines:
  org-clock is configured but unused; time is expressed as state
  transitions, Jira-style, each logged with a timestamp.
- Column view: #+COLUMNS ITEM TODO PRIORITY IMPACT Effort CLOCKSUM DEADLINE
  SCHEDULED TAGS (org-columns = bulk triage by single keys); CLOCKSUM
  reads empty, since no clock runs.
- File header per (todo) file: #+CATEGORY (used for agenda grouping and the
  queue's glut penalty), Effort_ALL, COLUMNS.  Tools: zetta-org-todo-census,
  zetta-org-insert-todo-header, zetta-org-migrate-checkboxes (one file at a
  time, NOT yet run on the real files -- they are still checkbox lists).

** Containers (all plain text, all in the rolo corpus, all indexed by irs)
- ~/kb/inbox.org -- the single inbox.
- ~/kb/todo/(todo) {emacs, emacs - categorized, buy, sell, calendar, email,
  interviews, todo, archive}.org -- flat per-domain task files = categories.
- ~/kb/notes/ -- 24 area/resource notes: schedule.org (the daily routine),

- ~/kb/wiki/ -- HyWiki pages (4 so far).  ~/kb/readwise/, ~/kb/org-remark/,
  ~/kb/pdf/ (pdfnote highlights), ~/kb/llm-convo/, ~/kb/yt-transcript/,

- ~/.private.el adds project files (e.g. blog-posts.org) to the agenda.
- testdata/todo/ -- a 94-entry synthetic corpus; zetta-org-toggle-todo-source
  flips agenda + rolo between test and real (never both).
- The hub auto-commits ~/kb daily ("auto: <date>"): a git timeline of every
  file in the kb.

** Choose (planning views)
- org-queue-today (",-o-Q"): commitments first (NEXT, scheduled today,
  deadline <= today, appointment today) and never scored; then candidates
  scored = convex deadline urgency (soft x0.35) + priority + IMPACT + log-age
  + PROG boost + quick-win (<=15m) + carry-over - category glut; QUES docked;
  HOLD/IDEA excluded; WAIT only inside a 7-day deadline horizon; optional
  context/energy filters; packed to a per-weekday capacity (guessed: 5h
  Mon-Thu, 4h Fri, 1.5h weekend) with 20% slack, WIP limit 3 PROG, default
  effort 30m marked as guessed; a second pass pulls future-scheduled work
  forward if the day underfills; every task lands in exactly one of planned
  / cut / dropped / deferred WITH A REASON; carry-over history persisted;
  estimates scaled by per-category calibration of actuals vs Effort
  (the harvest reads CLOCK lines, which are empty here; G15 re-points it
  at intervals derived from state transitions).
  org-queue-plan takes a date.  41 batch ERT tests over the pure core.
- org-agenda (",-o-v"): d Day (Next / Overdue / Due today / Appointments /
  Scheduled / Carried over / In progress / Waiting / Due this week / Parked,
  then a Backlog block: Next / PROG / Blocked / Quick / Top priority / by
  category); w Week (no grouping, by design); c Context (by @tag, catch-all
  "No context tag yet"); i Inbox; p Projects (by parent heading); n Neglected
  (touched this week / month / 1-3 months / >3 months / never -- age = newest
  timestamp anywhere in the entry incl. LOGBOOK); W Chase (WAIT/QUES by
  WAITING_ON then by age); I Parked (HOLD/IDEA, "REVIEW_ON has arrived"
  first); E Capacity (effort buckets); h Horizon (deadline distance);
  P Priority (A/B/C/never ranked); f Areas (by category); r Review (last 7
  days in log mode: Finished / Moved); b Blocked; u Untriaged (no estimate /
  no priority / no date).
- org-other-agenda board (",-o-b") reuses the Day command's blocks; calfw
  month grid; org-timegrid SVG week (drag = a real timestamped block written
  into (todo) calendar.org).
- HyRolo browse: ",-o-V" zetta-org-todo-rolo (consult-driven ripgrep over
  the agenda files, entries assembled with their subtrees; C-u = whole open
  backlog); ",-o-d" zetta-hyrolo-day (every entry mentioning a day, in both
  the ISO and the =[[Sep 9th, 2026]]= journal forms).

** Do
- The routine (~/kb/notes/schedule.org, 2026-08-11): 05:00 clean, 05:30 plan
  the day ("list tasks, delegate them to the focus blocks"), 06:00 lift,
  08:00-12:00 focus block 1 (pomodoros first 2h, vague second 2h), 12:00
  bike, 13:30-17:00 focus block 2 (pomodoros THROUGH the post-lunch dip,
  vague after 15:00), 18:00-20:00 admin/shallow overflow, 20:00 read (no

  budget not a well (4h max per block); order before focus; interviews are

* Appendix D — Method, and what could not be verified

** How this document was produced

Seven research agents each wrote one brief against primary sources, in a
fixed template (essence, primitives, rituals, rules of thumb, bookkeeping
cost, failure modes, unique contribution, signals to automate), so the
briefs could be read against one another.  Two analysis passes then ran
over the briefs and the config inventory: one produced the concept
crosswalk, the convergent core, the tensions and the composite; one
condensed every system into Part 1's shape.  Two further passes produced
the mapping (Part 3.1 to 3.5) and the decoration contract with its test
catalogue (Part 3.6 to 3.8).  The editor assembled and cut.  The
research briefs live only in the session's scratch directory; what
survives of them is here and in Appendix C's URLs.

The first draft assumed org-clock was in use.  The user corrected that
the same evening: time is expressed as TODO state transitions,
Jira-style, and the clock is to be assembled from them as a build of
their own.  G15 was rewritten around that, and every place that had
leaned on CLOCK lines (the calibration, the close, the review pack, the
day log, the timer, the feeds, the package verdicts) was re-pointed at
the derived intervals.

A second correction, the same evening: the ask read "writing tests" in
the first draft and should have read "writing tasks".  C1 now carries
the requirement (the work in your own words is your only writing); C7
and the introduction to 3.8 say plainly that the test catalogue is the
repo's convention, not the requirement; nothing in Part 2 or in the gap
mechanisms depended on the misreading.

A third revision, later the same day, on the follow-up that most code
is now written by agents kicked off and reviewed asynchronously, several
at a time: Part 2 gained the concept entry (convergence zero) and the
tension, the composite gained the Chain primitive and rules 13 and 14,
Part 3 gained G16 with the =AGENT= state, the landing log and the chain
limit, and the cadence gained kickoff, landing and priming.  Decisions
taken in conversation: interactive sessions by day and headless only
for unattended overnight runs; landing into =NEXT=; the Stop hook writes
a log rather than evaluating elisp; the prompt is the entry body or its
own sub-heading.

** Claims the briefs could not confirm

Carried here rather than smoothed over.

- Newport: "simulate a small shop / quota" and "planning is not the same
  as scheduling" appear in no primary source found; the attested forms
  are the pull system, projects limited by calendar capacity, and the
  weekly template's quota rules.  "Do something hard" is a podcast gloss.
  His seasonal leisure-plan dates are from one summary.
- Personal Kanban's "pen" was reached only through a summary calling it
  the "Waiting Space"; whether penned work counts against WIP is
  unverified.
- The 12 Week Year's execution threshold is 85% in the book and quoted as
  80% on the company's site; treat 80 to 85 as the figure.
- The Buffett 5/25 story is, in Burkeman's own words, "probably
  apocryphal"; the report of Buffett's denial was paywalled.
- Eisenhower never drew the grid; he was quoting a college president.
  The Ivy Lee story cannot be traced before 1960.  Tracy's Twain
